Chapter 01

Sanger sequencing and trace interpretation

Sanger sequencing reads a selected stretch of DNA, making it useful for checking an insert, a junction or a suspected base change. A primer starts DNA synthesis. Most added nucleotides allow extension to continue, but a dideoxynucleotide lacks the 3′ hydroxyl needed for the next addition. Its incorporation ends that copy. Fluorescent labels identify the terminal bases, and separation of the differently sized fragments reveals their order. The output is a sequence accompanied by a chromatogram showing the underlying signals.

From a DNA template to a chromatogram

  1. Choose the region and reading direction. A sequencing reaction uses a single primer that binds beside the region of interest. Polymerase extends from its 3′ end, so the primer position determines which region is read and in which direction. Longer regions require overlapping reads from additional primers.

  2. Generate a family of terminated strands. Ordinary nucleotides and fluorescently labelled ddNTPs are incorporated into newly synthesised copies. Different copies terminate at different positions. All begin from the same primer, but their ends form a ladder of successive lengths. The missing 3′ hydroxyl stops extension; the dye identifies the terminating base.

  3. Separate fragments and detect their labels. Capillary electrophoresis resolves the fragments by length, with shorter fragments detected before longer ones. Reading the terminal labels in that order gives the newly synthesised sequence in the 5′→3′ direction.

  4. Turn fluorescence into base calls. Software assigns bases to the peaks and estimates their quality. The trace file, commonly an .ab1 file, retains information that a plain sequence string omits: peak shape, separation and competing signals. Addgene: sequence analysis.

Inspect the trace, then align the read

Use well-separated peaks and low background to identify the dominant readable sequence. The beginning and end are often less reliable, so restrict the comparison to interpretable sequence. Align that region to the expected reference, checking mismatches, gaps and coverage. For an opposite-direction read, use the reverse complement so both sequences describe the same orientation.

A clear isolated peak is easier to call than overlapping peaks. Widespread overlap can arise from mixed templates or other sequencing problems; shifted overlap after a particular position can fit a mixture containing an insertion or deletion. Inspect the pattern before choosing an explanation. Yale: trace interpretation.

Controls and useful corroboration

A known-template control checks sequencing-reaction performance. A sequencing standard helps separate instrument problems from reaction-chemistry problems. For an important mismatch, an opposite-direction read provides corroboration at that position. Record exactly which insert regions and junctions the reads cover. Applied Biosystems: sequencing controls.

Hypothetical example

A benign reporter plasmid gives the expected-size PCR band, but a high-quality read shows G where the reference has A. The opposite-direction read agrees after reverse complementation. This identifies a substitution in the covered region. The gel result remains consistent because a base substitution preserves fragment length.

Explain the method: short oral answer

Question: How do terminated fragments become a Sanger sequence, and how would you analyse the read?

A single primer starts synthesis, and an incorporated ddNTP ends a copy because it lacks a 3′ hydroxyl. Capillary electrophoresis orders the fragments by length, and their fluorescent labels reveal the base order. I inspect the chromatogram, retain reliable sequence, align it in the correct orientation and examine mismatches and coverage.

Chapter 02

Gibson assembly and other assembly logic

Molecular cloning places a chosen DNA fragment in a carrier, often a plasmid, so a defined construct can be maintained and studied. Assembly is the joining step. In Gibson assembly, complementary sequence overlaps determine which fragments are neighbours. A 5′ exonuclease exposes those overlaps, DNA polymerase fills missing stretches, and DNA ligase seals breaks in the backbone. The fragment ends therefore encode the intended DNA arrangement before the enzymes join them.

From a construct map to joined DNA

  1. Define the intended arrangement. Draw the backbone, inserts, orientations and junctions. The backbone supplies the carrier; the inserts supply the sequences being investigated. If a coding sequence is fused to another sequence, the junction must preserve the intended reading frame. This map becomes the reference for later screening and sequencing.

  2. Specify the neighbours. In Gibson assembly, adjacent fragments share matching sequence at their ends. A fragment's exposed overlap should pair with its intended neighbour rather than another fragment. Overlap identity, rather than a required restriction recognition site, specifies the junction.

  3. Expose, pair and repair. The exonuclease removes nucleotides from 5′ ends, exposing complementary single-stranded regions that anneal. Polymerase fills gaps using the opposing strand as template. Ligase seals nicks between adjacent nucleotides. A gap contains missing nucleotides; a nick is a backbone break with no missing bases. Gibson et al.: assembly mechanism.

  4. Recover and identify candidates. Assembled DNA is introduced into a suitable benign host to recover individual candidate constructs. Screening narrows the candidates by insert size or junction arrangement. Sequence reads are then compared with the planned map, including the joins and important internal regions.

Joining specificity in other methods

Method Source of joining specificity
Restriction and ligation Cleavage creates compatible ends. Distinct, non-interchangeable ends can constrain orientation.
Gibson assembly Complementary overlaps guide fragment pairing. Junctions can be made without obligatory extra recognition-site sequence.
Golden Gate Type IIS enzymes cut outside their recognition sequences, generating overhangs that specify neighbours and orientation.

Choose the logic to suit the design: preserving a particular junction, reusing standard parts or combining several fragments. In Golden Gate, the enzyme recognition site and the overhang that joins the fragments are different features. Addgene: cloning comparisons.

Controls distinguish assembly from background

A backbone-only control estimates background candidates recovered without the intended insert. In restriction-ligation cloning, this can reveal residual uncut vector or vector recircularisation. An intact reference plasmid checks downstream recovery separately from the joining reaction. Subsequent PCR controls test the screening assay, while sequencing checks the candidate itself. Addgene: ligation controls.

Hypothetical example

A fluorescent-reporter construct requires backbone, promoter and reporter in a planned order. Gibson overlaps encode those neighbours. A candidate gives the expected flanking-PCR size, and reads covering the planned joins match the map. The construct is ready for a separate reporter assay, which measures its biological output.

Explain the method: short oral answer

Question: How do Gibson enzymes assemble DNA, and how does its joining logic differ from Golden Gate?

Gibson uses complementary overlaps to specify neighbouring fragments. A 5′ exonuclease exposes single-stranded ends, polymerase fills gaps after the overlaps anneal, and ligase seals nicks. Golden Gate instead uses type IIS restriction enzymes to generate designed overhangs outside their recognition sites. I would map the intended arrangement, screen recovered candidates and sequence the important regions and junctions.

Chapter 03

PCR, gels and clone verification

PCR selectively amplifies a DNA region so it can be detected, compared or examined further. A pair of primers binds to opposite strands around the region of interest. Repeated strand separation, primer binding and polymerase extension generate more copies, which themselves become templates. A conventional endpoint PCR is often read on an agarose gel. The primers determine which region is sampled, while the gel reports the approximate lengths of the amplified products. Together they make PCR a useful screening tool.

Follow the amplification cycle

  1. Define the amplicon. The amplicon is the DNA product bounded by the primer sites. The primers bind opposite strands with their extendable 3′ ends directed into the region being copied. Primers within an insert test for insert sequence; primers flanking it compare product size; a backbone primer paired with an insert primer can test a junction or orientation.

  2. Separate the strands. Denaturation disrupts base pairing in double-stranded DNA. This exposes the sequences to which the primers can bind. A heat-stable DNA polymerase allows the reaction to undergo repeated heating and cooling.

  3. Anneal the primers. Cooling allows complementary primer and template sequences to pair. Primers supply the starting points for synthesis and much of the assay's specificity. Binding to an unintended site can generate an additional or misleading product.

  4. Extend and repeat. Polymerase adds ordinary nucleotides to each primer, synthesising DNA in the 5′→3′ direction. The reaction contains template, primers, polymerase, nucleotide building blocks and a suitable buffer. Newly made strands become templates in later cycles, allowing exponential accumulation during efficient amplification. Addgene: PCR principles.

Read the products on a gel

DNA is negatively charged and moves towards the positive electrode through an agarose matrix. Shorter fragments generally migrate faster than longer ones. A DNA ladder supplies fragments of known lengths, allowing an approximate size estimate for each sample band. Addgene: gel electrophoresis.

Compare the observed pattern with the products predicted from the primer positions. A single expected-size band is a useful candidate-screening result. Additional bands suggest additional amplified products. Endpoint brightness reflects accumulated product, rather than serving as a simple measure of starting DNA. Sequencing supplies the base-level check after size-based screening.

Controls make the pattern interpretable

  • Known positive template: checks that the primer pair and amplification system produce the expected product.
  • No-template control: reveals contamination or primer-generated signal in the absence of added template.
  • Empty-vector or target-negative template: shows the expected negative pattern and helps test discrimination between the alternatives.
  • DNA ladder: provides the size reference, rather than testing reaction success.

Addgene: colony-PCR screening.

Hypothetical example

In a separate example, flanking primers produce 200 bp from an empty vector. Adding a 600 bp insert predicts an 800 bp product. Candidate C and the positive control show an 800 bp band; candidate D and the empty-vector control show 200 bp; the no-template lane is blank. C is the insert-sized candidate to sequence, while D resembles the empty vector.

Explain the method: short oral answer

Question: What happens during a PCR cycle, and how do primer placement and gel controls guide clone screening?

Denaturation separates the DNA strands, annealing lets the primers bind, and extension makes new strands that become templates in later cycles. Primer positions define the expected product and whether I am testing insert presence, size or a junction. I compare gel bands with a ladder, positive template, negative template and no-template control, then sequence the selected candidate.

Chapter 04

qPCR, RT-qPCR and normalisation

Quantitative PCR follows DNA amplification through fluorescence recorded during successive cycles. A DNA-binding dye or a sequence-specific probe connects product accumulation to an optical signal. An assay with more starting target generally reaches a measurable signal earlier, provided its amplification behaviour is comparable. RT-qPCR adds reverse transcription: RNA is first copied into complementary DNA, which becomes the PCR template. The analysis turns amplification curves into target quantities or normalised comparisons, rather than treating the final fluorescence as the starting amount.

From the sample to an amplification curve

  1. Define what is being measured. qPCR can start from DNA. For an RNA-expression question, reverse transcription produces cDNA, making RNA quality and the reverse-transcription step part of the measurement. Keep independent biological samples identifiable; repeated wells from the same sample assess technical repeatability.

  2. Connect amplification to fluorescence. A DNA-binding dye reports double-stranded product. A hydrolysis probe binds within the target amplicon and carries a reporter and quencher. During extension, probe cleavage separates the reporter from the quencher, allowing fluorescence to be detected. The dye and probe approaches therefore generate signal by different mechanisms. EMBL-EBI: real-time PCR.

  3. Review curves before extracting quantities. An amplification curve progresses from background through rapid product accumulation towards a plateau. Cq is a value estimated on the cycle scale. With a threshold-based method, it is the cycle at which the curve crosses a chosen threshold; other algorithms estimate it differently. Compare the same assay using a consistent analysis, and examine unusual curves or discordant technical wells.

  4. Account for amplification efficiency. Efficiency describes product increase per cycle. Baseline estimation and inhibition can affect the apparent curve and the calculated quantity. A calibration series or an appropriate curve-analysis method helps characterise assay performance. MIQE 2.0 recommends converting Cq values into efficiency-corrected target quantities. Ruijter et al.: efficiency; MIQE 2.0.

Controls and normalisation have different jobs

A no-template control checks for signal without added template, including contamination or primer-related products. A no-RT control in an RNA workflow checks for amplification from DNA carried through the preparation. For dye-based assays, a melting curve helps assess product consistency; sequence identity requires a sequence-specific check.

A reference target provides an internal normalisation denominator and should be stable in the comparison. The calibrator is the sample or condition against which normalised values are expressed. These are different roles.

Hypothetical comparative calculation

For a teaching example, assume near-doubling amplification and comparable target and reference efficiencies:

  • Control: target Cq = 25, reference Cq = 20, so ΔCq = 5.
  • Test: target Cq = 23, reference Cq = 20, so ΔCq = 3.
  • ΔΔCq = ΔCq(test) − ΔCq(control) = −2.
  • Relative quantity = 2^(−ΔΔCq) = 4.

The test therefore has fourfold higher normalised target in this model. ΔCq performs within-sample normalisation; ΔΔCq compares the normalised samples. For measured efficiencies that differ from these assumptions, use an appropriate efficiency-aware calculation. Livak and Schmittgen: comparative method.

Explain the method: short oral answer

Question: How does RT-qPCR turn RNA into a normalised comparison between conditions?

Reverse transcription copies RNA into cDNA. PCR then amplifies a selected target while a dye or probe reports product accumulation. I inspect the curves, check no-template and no-RT controls, and account for amplification efficiency. A stable reference normalises within each sample, and a calibrator defines the between-condition comparison. Technical wells assess repeatability; biological samples provide independent replication.

Chapter 05

FISH and spatial evidence

Fluorescence in situ hybridisation, or FISH, locates selected DNA or RNA sequences in preserved cells or tissues. A labelled nucleic-acid probe pairs with a complementary target, and fluorescence microscopy shows where the bound probe is located. DNA FISH can reveal chromosome regions or loci; RNA FISH shows the distribution of selected transcripts. Because the sample remains spatially organised, the method connects molecular detection with individual cells and cellular compartments, adding information that is lost when a sample is pooled for extraction.

From a preserved sample to a spatial signal

  1. Choose the target and probe. Complementarity supplies sequence recognition. A DNA probe can identify a genomic region, while an RNA-targeting probe set can reveal transcript location. Probe choice therefore defines both the molecular question and the signal being interpreted. NHGRI: DNA FISH.

  2. Preserve structure and expose the target. Fixation retains the specimen's organisation, while permeabilisation allows probes to enter cells. In conventional DNA FISH, denaturation separates the target DNA strands so a complementary probe can bind. Target accessibility matters: a complementary sequence must also be physically available to the probe. Preparation can affect both morphology and detection. FISH workflow; Fuchs et al.: accessibility.

  3. Hybridise and wash. Probes pair with accessible complementary regions. Washing removes excess probe and reduces unwanted signal, improving separation of retained target-associated fluorescence from background. The useful readout is the fluorescence pattern after this sequence of sample preparation, binding and washing.

  4. Image and assign signals to structures. Acquire the relevant fluorescence channels and identify cells, nuclei or other compartments. Image segmentation defines those boundaries computationally or manually. Where depth matters, inspect the focal planes as well as any projection. Keep acquisition and analysis comparable across the samples being contrasted.

Controls separate detection from background

A known-positive specimen containing the target checks that the probe and detection workflow can produce the expected signal. A target-negative specimen processed with the same probes helps reveal background and off-target binding. For multicolour imaging, single-colour controls help check whether signal leaks into another channel.

Interpret colour overlap at the resolution of the image. Optical spread and projection through depth can make separate structures appear coincident, so apparent overlap indicates proximity on that imaging scale rather than physical binding.

Decide what to measure

Useful outputs include the fraction of signal-positive cells, signal location, fluorescence intensity and spots per cell. In validated single-molecule RNA FISH, multiple probes make individual transcripts visible as detectable spots. Spot counting still requires reliable detection and cell assignment; conventional FISH brightness is not automatically a molecule count. Raj et al.: single-molecule RNA imaging.

Hypothetical example

In a validated spot-counting assay, sample A has ten cells with two spots each. Sample B has two cells with ten spots each and eight with none. Both contain twenty spots and average two per cell, but B concentrates the signal in a minority of cells. A pooled abundance measurement could look similar while FISH reveals the different distribution.

Explain the method: short oral answer

Question: How does FISH turn sequence complementarity into spatial information, and what is analysed in the images?

A labelled probe hybridises to its complementary DNA or RNA target in a preserved specimen. Preparation makes the target accessible, washing reduces excess probe, and microscopy records where signal remains. I use positive and negative specimens to assess detection and background, then assign signals to cells or compartments and compare location, positive-cell fractions, intensity or validated spot counts.

Read further: Amann et al.: oligonucleotide probes in intact cells · Fuchs et al.: target accessibility.

Chapter 06

CRISPR-Cas and levels of evidence

CRISPR-Cas systems give many bacteria and archaea a sequence-based defence against invading genetic material. A cell stores short fragments of foreign DNA, makes RNA guides from that record, and uses Cas proteins to recognise matching targets. The Cas9 research tool borrows this recognition mechanism: guide RNA directs a nuclease to DNA beside a compatible PAM, and cellular repair acts on the break. Understanding these stages separates target recognition from a lasting sequence change and from gene inactivation.

Natural immunity in three stages

A CRISPR array is a stretch of DNA containing repeated sequences separated by variable spacers. Cas means CRISPR-associated. Cas9 belongs to one branch of this larger family, the type II systems.

  1. Adaptation records an encounter. A fragment of foreign DNA can become a new spacer in the array. The stored sequence provides a heritable record against which later invading material can be recognised.
  2. Expression produces the guides. The array is transcribed, and the precursor RNA is processed into short CRISPR RNAs, or crRNAs. In the Cas9 system, a trans-activating RNA, tracrRNA, pairs with the crRNA and helps form the functional guide complex.
  3. Interference acts on a matching target. The Cas9-RNA complex recognises a compatible protospacer-adjacent motif, or PAM, in the target DNA. The guide can base-pair with a neighbouring complementary sequence. Cas9's HNH and RuvC nuclease domains cut opposite DNA strands.

The spacer in the array is the stored sequence; the corresponding foreign target sequence is the protospacer. The PAM is a feature of the target DNA, not part of the RNA guide. Different Cas systems recognise and process targets differently.

What the Cas9 tool borrows

A research single-guide RNA, or sgRNA, joins the targeting and structural functions of crRNA and tracrRNA in one molecule. Its targeting region supplies sequence complementarity, while its scaffold supports interaction with Cas9.

In the common nuclease-based editing model, recognition leads to a double-strand break. The cell then repairs that break. End joining reconnects DNA ends and can leave insertions or deletions; template-directed repair can copy information from homologous DNA. Cas9 makes the break, while cellular repair determines the resulting sequence. Repair can also restore the original sequence.

An insertion or deletion does not automatically inactivate a gene. Its position and effect on the gene product matter. A knockout genetically inactivates a gene; a knockdown reduces its expression or product.

Reading an experiment

Measurement What it establishes
Cas9 protein detection The tool is detectable
Target-region sequencing Which sequence variants are present in the measured region
RNA, protein or functional measurement The corresponding downstream consequence

Matched untreated and handling controls establish baseline effects. A non-targeting guide can control aspects of tool delivery, but it does not reproduce another guide's off-target sites. Independent biological samples test reproducibility.

Hypothetical example. In a reporter study, Cas9 is detected, sequencing reveals both unchanged and altered target sequences, and average fluorescence falls. Together, these observations support a sequence change in part of the sampled population and reduced reporter output. They do not establish that every cell has a fully inactive reporter. The sequence mixture and the functional readout answer different parts of that question.

Explain the method: short oral answer

Question: How does Cas9 recognise and cut DNA, and why does cleavage not guarantee a knockout?

Cas9 works with a guide RNA. A compatible PAM in the DNA permits target recognition, and guide complementarity helps specify the neighbouring sequence. Cas9's nuclease domains cut the two strands. Cellular repair then determines the final sequence. A repaired allele may remain functional, so a knockout requires evidence of genetic inactivation and its relevant consequence.

Chapter 07

ELISA, cytokines and detection

An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, or ELISA, uses antibody binding to connect a target molecule to a measurable reporter signal. In a sandwich ELISA, an immobilised capture antibody retains the target, and a second antibody recognises another accessible epitope, the part of the molecule it binds. An enzyme-associated detection system generates a signal that is compared with standards of known concentration. This makes ELISA useful for estimating a cytokine or other protein in a complex sample.

From antibody binding to concentration

A sandwich needs two antibodies that can bind the target at the same time. This gives the assay two recognition steps. The reader measures reporter output, usually absorbance in a colourimetric assay, rather than counting cytokine molecules directly.

  1. Capture and blocking define the surface. Capture antibody is attached to the plate. Blocking occupies remaining binding sites, reducing unwanted attachment of sample proteins and detection reagents.
  2. Samples and standards supply the target. Target molecules bind to the capture antibody. Standards contain known amounts of the reference analyte and pass through the same detection stages as the unknown samples. Washing removes material that has not remained bound.
  3. The detection antibody identifies captured target. It binds another accessible epitope. Its reporter may be attached directly or recruited by an additional binding reagent. Washing removes unbound detector, reducing signal unrelated to retained target.
  4. The reporter makes binding measurable. An enzyme converts its substrate into a detectable product. For example, HRP can generate a coloured product from TMB. Comparable development conditions matter because the readout depends on product formation as well as retained enzyme.
  5. Calibration converts signal into an estimate. Apply the assay's background correction and fitted standard curve, then account for sample dilution. Use the supported quantification range. The relationship need not be linear, particularly near the ends of the curve.

Controls that answer different questions

A zero-analyte standard or reagent blank measures background from the assay system. A sample-matrix blank, when available, checks background contributed by the medium or other sample components. A known positive control checks that the detection chain works.

Technical replicate wells reveal variation in dispensing and detection; independent biological samples reveal variation between the biological units being compared. They are not interchangeable. If the matrix affects recovery, a known-analyte spike tests the response to added target. Agreement between dilution-corrected estimates tests whether sample dilution behaves consistently.

A hypothetical readout

Suppose a sample diluted fourfold gives a signal that the accepted standard curve maps to 37.5 pg/mL, within this hypothetical assay's quantification range. Its estimated original concentration is 37.5 × 4 = 150 pg/mL. If the corresponding dilution-corrected control is 75 pg/mL, the sample estimate is twofold higher. These numbers illustrate the calculation, not a kit specification.

For a culture supernatant, this result describes detectable extracellular cytokine at the sampling time. Its concentration reflects secretion, uptake or breakdown, the number of contributing cells and medium volume. Cell abundance and sampling context therefore help interpret the comparison. Cytokine concentration alone measures neither a production rate nor biological activity. Below the lower quantification limit, report the appropriate limit category rather than assigning zero or an unsupported precise concentration.

Explain the method: short oral answer

Question: How does a sandwich ELISA turn antibody binding into an estimate of cytokine concentration?

The plate-bound antibody captures the cytokine, and a second antibody binds another accessible epitope. An enzyme-associated reporter produces a measurable signal after unbound reagents are washed away. Standards connect that signal to concentration within the assay's quantification range. I then apply the sample dilution factor and report extracellular concentration, rather than treating it as a direct production-rate measurement.

How the main ELISA formats are arranged

The format describes how the target and antibodies are attached. The reporter describes how binding becomes a measurable signal.

Indirect antigen-down detection Antigen On a secondary antibody that recognises the primary
Sandwich with a labelled detector Capture antibody, followed by captured target On a second target-binding antibody

A sandwich uses two antibodies that bind accessible sites on the same analyte. HRP can be used in several formats, so identifying the binding layers is the clearest way to describe an assay.

How a competitive assay works

In the illustrated format, sample analyte competes with a labelled analogue for a limited number of binding sites.

  1. More sample analyte occupies more of the available binding sites.
  2. Less labelled analogue remains bound.
  3. The measured signal decreases as sample analyte concentration increases.

This is useful when the target has only one accessible antibody-binding site. The curve direction follows the species being detected: here, the reporter measures bound labelled analogue.

How HRP-TMB generates colour

HRP is an enzyme attached to a detection reagent. It uses hydrogen peroxide to oxidise TMB, producing a coloured product. In a common colourimetric system, the product is blue before acid stopping and yellow afterwards; the stopped assay is typically read near 450 nm.

The measurement chain is: retained detector → reporter enzyme → coloured product → absorbance. Standards measured under matching conditions convert absorbance into an estimate of analyte concentration.

Using the calibration curve

  1. Compare the sample signal with standards covering the supported measurement range.
  2. Convert an in-range signal into concentration, accounting for any sample dilution.
  3. Use dilutional agreement to check whether back-calculated concentrations remain consistent. Spike recovery checks how a known addition is recovered in the sample matrix.

Near a plateau, large concentration differences can produce similar signals. At very high analyte levels, some sandwich assays show a high-dose hook effect, where signal falls. Below the quantification limit, report the appropriate range or detection category rather than a precise concentration.

Hypothetical example

Two in-range samples contain an estimated 30 and 45 pg/mL of detectable analyte, a difference of 15 pg/mL. For a cell-culture experiment, compare samples collected at the same time and describe the cell number and medium volume. The concentration reflects the balance of secretion, uptake and breakdown. The chapters on protein secretion and enzyme kinetics explain these processes further.

Your assay uses HRP. How would you tell direct antigen-down detection from a sandwich with an HRP-labelled detector?

I would identify what is immobilised. In direct antigen-down detection, antigen is on the plate and an HRP-labelled primary binds it. In a sandwich, the plate has a capture antibody, and a second antigen-specific antibody can carry HRP directly. Both can use the same reporter.

Follow-up: What changes if the detector carries biotin instead of HRP?

Listen for: Streptavidin–HRP binds biotin on the detector; the capture format can remain a sandwich.

Two samples have nearly identical absorbance near the upper plateau. Are they equally concentrated?

Not necessarily. The response changes little with concentration near saturation, so the readout may not resolve a difference. I would seek measurements in the supported range and check agreement after accounting for dilution and matrix effects.

Follow-up: How is the high-dose hook effect different from saturation?

Listen for: In some sandwich formats extreme analyte can lower, not merely flatten, signal; low signal is not always low analyte.

In a competitive assay, why can a darker result mean less sample target?

In the labelled-tracer design, sample target competes with labelled tracer for binding sites. The reporter measures bound tracer, not unlabelled sample target. If more tracer remains bound, there may have been less sample target under the assay’s calibrated conditions.

Follow-up: Would every assay called competitive necessarily show the same curve direction?

Listen for: Identify the detected species and assay geometry; explain the direction for that design.

Chapter 08

Flow cytometry and Annexin V/PI

Flow cytometry measures light scatter and fluorescence as particles pass through laser illumination. Fluorescent antibodies or dyes link those signals to cell features, so an analysis can describe both population frequencies and fluorescence distributions. Each recorded event is initially a detected particle, not necessarily one intact cell. Gating selects the events relevant to the question. In an Annexin V/propidium iodide assay, the final fluorescence patterns describe phosphatidylserine accessibility and membrane permeability within that selected population.

What the instrument records

The fluidics system carries the suspension past the laser. Optics collect scattered light and fluorescence in defined wavelength channels, and electronics convert detector signals into numerical values for each event. A histogram displays one parameter; a two-dimensional plot shows how two parameters vary together.

Forward scatter, FSC, and side scatter, SSC, help distinguish populations empirically. They often relate to cell size and structural complexity, but they are not universal measurements of diameter or granularity. Fluorescence intensity describes the detected label signal; a positive-cell percentage describes how many events cross a chosen boundary.

Build the denominator before calculating a percentage

A typical hierarchy moves from broad event selection to the biological endpoint:

  1. Select the relevant particle population. An FSC/SSC gate can reduce debris while retaining the cell population of interest. Check whether treatment shifts its scatter profile before applying a gate that could exclude affected cells.
  2. Enrich for single cells. Compare pulse area with height or width to distinguish singlet-like events from aggregates. For example, FSC-A versus FSC-H uses the relationship between integrated scatter and peak scatter.
  3. Define the biological parent population. In a mixed sample, marker gates can identify the relevant cell type. Apply the endpoint gates within that parent and state that denominator when reporting percentages.

For Annexin V/PI analysis, do not first discard PI-positive events with a routine live-cell gate: membrane permeability is part of the endpoint being measured.

Set fluorescence boundaries with controls

An unstained sample reveals autofluorescence and background. Single-stain controls estimate how each fluorophore spills into other detectors for compensation in conventional multicolour cytometry. Compensation corrects signal overlap; it does not remove the associated spread of population measurements.

A fluorescence-minus-one, or FMO, control contains the other panel labels but omits the label being assessed. It helps place a gate when background and spillover spread blur the boundary. It cannot replace single-stain compensation controls or establish antibody specificity. Biological negative and positive controls establish how the assay behaves in relevant samples.

Read Annexin V and PI together

Annexin V binds phosphatidylserine in a calcium-dependent interaction. PI enters membrane-compromised cells and stains nucleic acids.

Positive / positive Accessible phosphatidylserine and membrane damage; compatible with late apoptosis or other damage

Western blot and total protein

A western blot separates proteins, transfers them to a membrane, and uses antibodies to reveal a selected target. The band's position gives its apparent molecular mass, while a suitable signal can support a relative abundance comparison. Bradford complements this workflow by estimating total protein before loading. Membrane-level loading checks and normalisation address what happened after that initial measurement. Together, these steps connect a complex protein mixture to an interpretable target-band readout.

What each stage accomplishes

  1. SDS-PAGE separates the mixture. Sodium dodecyl sulfate, SDS, unfolds proteins and gives them a broadly similar negative charge-to-mass ratio. The polyacrylamide gel acts as a molecular sieve: smaller polypeptides generally migrate farther in the electric field. Reducing conditions also break disulfide bonds, separating disulfide-linked subunits. A protein ladder provides an apparent-mass reference, usually expressed in kilodaltons, kDa.
  2. Transfer makes separated proteins accessible. An electric field moves proteins from the gel onto a nitrocellulose or PVDF membrane while largely preserving their lane positions. A total-protein image of the membrane helps assess transfer and the protein pattern in each lane.
  3. Blocking reduces unwanted antibody binding. The membrane also binds proteins at unoccupied sites. Blocking occupies those sites so detection antibodies are less likely to generate background across the membrane.
  4. Antibodies select the target. A primary antibody recognises an epitope on the protein. A labelled secondary antibody commonly recognises the primary antibody's host species and immunoglobulin class. Washing removes loosely associated or unbound reagents. A directly labelled primary is another detection arrangement.
  5. Detection reveals antibody-associated signal. An enzyme-linked secondary can generate light through a substrate reaction, or a fluorescent label can be imaged directly. Band intensity depends on the detection system and image acquisition as well as target abundance. Quantitative comparisons need unsaturated measurements in the established response range.
  6. Densitometry turns the image into a comparison. Measure integrated signal over a consistently defined band region and subtract an appropriate local background. Divide each target signal by its corresponding lane reference, then express that ratio relative to the control. A validated total-protein signal or a demonstrably stable loading-control protein can provide the reference. Both target and reference need suitable quantitative ranges.

Where Bradford fits

The Bradford assay measures the colour change of Coomassie dye bound to proteins. Comparison with a protein standard estimates total protein concentration, allowing loading amounts to be planned rather than equalising lysate volumes blindly. Its response depends on protein composition and assay compatibility.

Bradford measures the sample before electrophoresis. It therefore complements, rather than replaces, a membrane-level loading and transfer check. A housekeeping protein is only a useful denominator if its abundance is stable in the comparison.

Controls and a hypothetical readout

A known-positive sample checks detection; a suitable target-negative sample helps assess band identity. Omitting the primary antibody tests secondary-associated background. The expected apparent mass is supporting information, not sufficient proof that an antibody recognises only the intended target. Independent biological samples establish whether the comparison is reproducible.

Suppose two lanes on the same blot give these background-corrected signals in arbitrary units, all within their validated ranges:

Lane Target Total-protein reference Target/reference
Control 1,200 600 2.0
Comparison 2,700 900 3.0

The relative normalised signal is 3.0 / 2.0 = 1.5-fold. This supports more detectable target relative to loaded total protein in the comparison lane. It does not measure the protein's enzymatic activity.

Explain the method: short oral answer

Question: How do SDS-PAGE, antibody detection, Bradford and normalisation contribute to a western blot comparison?

SDS-PAGE separates denatured polypeptides mainly by size, and transfer places them on a membrane for antibody detection. Bradford estimates total protein before loading. Densitometry measures background-corrected target-band signal, which I normalise to an appropriate lane reference and compare with control. I also check band specificity and the quantitative ranges of both signals before interpreting a relative abundance difference.

Experimental design and statistical contrasts

Experimental design connects a biological question to an informative comparison. Start by deciding what changes, what you will measure, and which independently sampled or assigned units support the comparison. Then arrange controls, replication and sample handling around that question. A useful design is not simply a large collection of measurements: it makes the biological effect distinguishable from donor differences, background signal and the circumstances in which samples were processed.

1. Turn the question into a measurable comparison

Suppose you ask whether a stimulus changes secretion of a signalling protein. Define the model, exposure comparison, sampling time and primary outcome before collecting data. For example, compare protein concentration in medium from stimulated and control cultures. Write the intended effect explicitly: the average stimulated-minus-control difference. This determines which samples, controls and analysis you need.

2. Identify the unit and preserve pairing

If each independent donor supplies both conditions, the donor provides a biological pair. Compare conditions within donors, then estimate the average paired response. This removes baseline donor differences from the treatment comparison. Repeated wells from the same sample assess measurement repeatability; they do not add independent donors. Keep their relationship to the original sample in the data table, and either summarise them appropriately or use an analysis that represents their nesting.

3. Give controls and sample allocation distinct jobs

Design element What it contributes
Negative control Characterises background
Positive control Checks detection of an expected signal
Vehicle control Separates the stimulus from its solvent
Randomisation Assigns conditions or processing order without systematic preference
Blocking Groups comparable units, such as samples from one donor or day
Blinding Reduces expectation-driven measurement or scoring bias

Include both conditions within relevant blocks, rather than processing all controls on one day and all stimulated samples on another.

4. Match the contrast to the biological question

A contrast is the particular difference the analysis estimates. For a time-course question, compare changes rather than just final values. If group A changes from 10 to 16 and group B from 10 to 14, their changes are 6 and 4; the difference in changes is 2. Its uncertainty comes from the replicate data and design. Likewise, comparing A with control and B with control does not replace the direct A-versus-B contrast.

5. Plan replication and report the effect

Choose sample size around a biologically meaningful difference, expected variability and the planned analysis. Prioritise independent biological replication over repeatedly measuring the same material. Present the effect estimate with its units and an uncertainty interval; show individual biological observations where practical. State what error bars represent. The result should tell readers both how large the response was and how precisely the study estimated it.

Explain the method: short oral answer

Question: Why pair samples by donor, and how is that pairing used in the analysis?

Pairing makes each donor their own comparator. I would calculate the test-minus-control response within each donor, then estimate the average paired response and its uncertainty. The donor supplies the independent biological pair. Repeated wells refine each donor's measurement; they do not create additional donors or replace the paired analysis.

Bioinformatics and bulk RNA sequencing

Bulk RNA sequencing measures RNA abundance across a sample and compares expression between biological conditions. Its central product is a gene-by-sample measurement matrix linked to a sample table. A differential expression workflow turns those measurements into estimated changes, uncertainty and biological interpretation. Each stage has a different purpose: prepare representative libraries, assess reads, quantify genes, make samples comparable, inspect their relationships, and fit the comparison specified by the study design.

1. Define the design and sample table

Imagine a hypothetical stimulus study in which three independent donors each provide control and stimulated cells. This is a paired illustration, not a sample-size recommendation. Give every library a unique identifier:

Sample Donor Condition
C1 D1 Control
S1 D1 Stimulus
C2 D2 Control
S2 D2 Stimulus
C3 D3 Control
S3 D3 Stimulus

Also record preparation batch, sample provenance and relevant covariates. Balance conditions across batches. Output: a sample table defining the biological replicates, pairing and intended stimulus-versus-control comparison.

2. Understand RNA-to-library conversion

After RNA extraction and quality assessment, library preparation enriches the RNA population of interest, for example through poly(A) selection or ribosomal RNA depletion. RNA is converted into a sequencing-compatible cDNA library, with fragmentation and adapter addition arranged according to the protocol. Sample indexes identify pooled libraries. Sequencing produces reads. Output: FASTQ files containing read sequences and per-base quality scores, linked to sample identifiers.

3. Inspect FASTQ quality

Examine base-quality profiles, adapter content, read lengths and sequence-composition patterns. FastQC summarises these features; reports across samples reveal unusually poor libraries or inconsistent processing. Trim adapters or poor-quality sequence when justified, then reassess. Base-call quality describes confidence in individual bases, not confidence in where a read maps. Output: quality reports and documented decisions about retained reads.

4. Map reads or quantify transcripts

Genome alignment places reads on a reference genome, including exon-junction reads through splice-aware mapping. It yields alignment files for inspection and counting. Transcript quantification, for example with Salmon, estimates transcript abundances against a transcript reference and accounts for fragments compatible with multiple transcripts. Output: genome alignments or transcript-level estimates, together with assignment and mapping summaries.

5. Build an annotated gene-level matrix

Annotation connects reference sequences to transcripts, exons and genes. Count aligned fragments against gene features, or import transcript estimates through a supported workflow such as tximport, which aggregates transcripts to genes and carries relevant length information into downstream analysis. Output: genes as rows, libraries as columns, with matching sample metadata and recorded reference and annotation versions.

6. Account for library scale and composition

Samples differ in sequencing depth and in which transcripts dominate the library. DESeq2 estimates sample-specific size factors from expression across many genes to establish a comparable relative scale. This relies on a sufficiently stable reference across genes. Output: scaling factors for the count model and normalised counts for summaries. TPM values are not substituted for this workflow's count input.

7. Transform expression for exploration

A variance-stabilising transformation reduces the strong dependence of variability on abundance. Use transformed values for PCA, sample-distance plots and heatmaps. PCA summarises major sample-level variation; heatmaps display patterns across selected genes. Colour samples by condition, donor and batch to interpret those patterns. Output: exploratory plots that connect expression structure to sample metadata.

8. Fit the model and specify the contrast

A DESeq2-style negative binomial model relates expected counts to library scale and experimental factors while estimating variability among replicates. For this paired example, ~ donor + condition represents donor baselines and the condition effect. Fit the model using counts or supported imported estimates, not the PCA matrix. Specify stimulus versus control explicitly. Output: gene-level effects, uncertainty and statistical tests.

9. Read log2 fold changes and FDR

For a hypothetical gene, an estimated stimulus/control ratio of 2 gives log2FC = +1; a ratio of 0.5 gives -1. The result table combines effect size, standard error, p-value and an adjusted p-value. An FDR procedure addresses multiple testing by controlling the expected false-discovery proportion among selected genes. A threshold of 0.05 is not a 5% error probability for each individual gene.

10. Connect genes to biological processes

Inspect representative genes alongside their expression patterns. Gene Ontology enrichment asks whether particular functions occur more often in a selected gene set than in an appropriate background, such as all tested genes. Correct gene-set tests for multiple comparisons too. Interpret bulk expression in the context of the sampled cell mixture. Output: a documented gene-result table and a biological summary grounded in coordinated expression changes.

Explain the method: short oral answer

Question: Why use transformed expression for PCA but counts for differential expression?

PCA compares sample relationships, so a variance-stabilising transformation helps prevent abundance-dependent variability from dominating the display. The differential expression model instead represents count variability and library scaling directly. I would therefore explore transformed values but fit the count-based model to counts or supported imported estimates, with the sample design and contrast specified.

Microbiome data, bacteriophages and AMR

Microbiome analysis, bacteriophage biology and antimicrobial resistance describe connected aspects of microbial life. Community surveys ask which organisms and genes are present. Phage biology explains how viruses interact with bacterial hosts. Resistance biology examines how bacteria maintain growth in the presence of antimicrobial drugs. Keeping these levels distinct makes the subject easier to navigate: community composition, viral life cycles and bacterial responses each require their own measurements and vocabulary.

1. Choose the community measurement

A 16S marker-gene survey profiles bacterial and archaeal membership through a shared taxonomic marker. Shotgun metagenomics samples DNA more broadly, supporting taxonomic profiling and analysis of encoded genes. Gene content describes functional potential rather than current gene expression. Both approaches produce community profiles whose resolution depends on the sequences recovered, analysis method and reference database.

2. Read abundance and diversity together

Relative abundance is a share of a total. In the hypothetical quantities shown here, a focal taxon remains at 20 units while the community total rises from 100 to 200. Its share falls from 20% to 10% without any decrease in its absolute amount. Sequencing depth alone does not measure the original microbial load; quantitative profiling needs an independent link to sample abundance.

Alpha diversity summarises diversity within a sample, such as richness or a measure incorporating evenness. Beta diversity describes differences between communities. Different metrics emphasise different features, so state which metric a plot represents.

3. Follow the phage life cycle

A bacteriophage is a virus that infects bacteria. In a lytic infection, the phage introduces its genome, produces new genomes and particles using the infected cell, and releases progeny through lysis. A temperate phage can instead establish lysogeny: its genome persists as a prophage associated with the host. Prophages are often integrated into the chromosome, although some persist as plasmid-like elements. They can later enter a productive lytic cycle.

Host prediction associates a phage sequence with a likely host group. Productive infection of an individual strain is a more specific biological property, which a broad taxonomic prediction does not settle.

4. Understand resistance mechanisms

Bacteria can resist antibiotics by inactivating a drug, altering its target, reducing entry or increasing efflux. Resistance traits can arise through mutations or be acquired through genetic exchange; descendants inherit traits that favour growth under particular selection conditions. Thus, resistance concerns a bacterial phenotype and its underlying biology, not simply the presence of a familiar gene name in a community profile.

5. Distinguish growth inhibition from killing

The minimum inhibitory concentration, or MIC, describes inhibition of visible growth under defined conditions. Survival over time addresses killing instead.

Term Central distinction
Resistance Growth at higher antibiotic concentrations, reflected in increased MIC under comparable conditions
Tolerance Slower killing without a corresponding MIC increase
Persistence A subpopulation displays prolonged survival without increased resistance
Heteroresistance A subpopulation has higher resistance than the main population

These terms separate mechanisms that can look similar if the only reported observation is that some cells survived. Clinical susceptibility categories additionally depend on appropriate interpretation criteria.

The prophage is often chromosomally integrated, but some are maintained as plasmid-like elements. A lysogenic state can later switch into a productive lytic cycle.

DNA replication and repair

DNA replication preserves sequence information by using each parental strand as a template for a new complementary strand. Each resulting duplex therefore contains one old strand and one new strand: semiconservative replication. The apparent asymmetry at a replication fork comes from a simple constraint. DNA polymerase extends an existing 3′ end, so both new strands grow 5′→3′ even though their templates run in opposite directions. Leading and lagging synthesis solve this geometrical problem with the same chemistry.

Follow the fork in order

Start by labelling both ends of each parental strand. For a fork moving right, the template running 3′→5′ towards the right supports continuous synthesis towards the fork. Its antiparallel partner supports synthesis away from the fork, in successive Okazaki fragments.

  1. Helicase separates the strands. Topoisomerases relieve torsional stress ahead of the fork, while single-strand-binding proteins protect the exposed templates.
  2. Primase supplies a short primer with an available 3′-OH. The leading strand needs an initial primer; each new lagging-strand fragment needs another.
  3. DNA polymerase adds nucleotides to the growing 3′ end. A sliding clamp helps it remain associated with DNA during extension.
  4. Primer processing removes the RNA and replaces it with DNA. Ligase seals the remaining nicks, completing the backbone. A nick is one missing backbone bond, whereas a gap lacks nucleotides. The proteins that process primers differ between bacteria and eukaryotes.

The lagging polymerase does not synthesise DNA backwards. Fork movement continually exposes more template, which requires repeated starts because extension must still run 5′→3′.

Correct an error at the appropriate stage

Proofreading checks the growing end during replication. A proofreading polymerase can remove a misincorporated nucleotide using 3′→5′ exonuclease activity, then resume 5′→3′ synthesis. Selecting the correct incoming nucleotide and removing an incorrect one are distinct contributions to accuracy.

Mismatch repair acts on some errors that escape proofreading. Other repair pathways recognise damage: base-excision repair typically starts by removing an altered base, while nucleotide-excision repair removes a short stretch around a bulky lesion. These systems recognise different starting problems, even though their repair products must ultimately restore an intact duplex.

A mismatch is an incorrectly paired intermediate. If it escapes correction, subsequent copying can establish a stable sequence change in one descendant lineage. This distinction connects an immediate copying error with an inherited mutation.

Worked conceptual example: follow the labelled strands

Imagine initially heavy-labelled DNA undergoing complete replication rounds in a light-labelled environment. Track which parental strands each model retains together.

Replication model After the first round After the second round
Semiconservative Hybrid duplexes Hybrid and light duplexes
Conservative Separate heavy and light duplexes Separate heavy and light duplexes
Classic dispersive One intermediate-density population One population shifted towards light density

The first intermediate band fits both semiconservative and classic dispersive replication. The second round separates their predictions: retaining an intact heavy parental strand produces a hybrid class alongside a light class. This is the logic of the Meselson–Stahl experiment. Density reveals how the label is distributed among DNA molecules; the fork machinery explains how copying accomplishes that distribution.

Oral practice

Why can leading-strand synthesis be continuous while the lagging strand is made in fragments?

Both DNA strands are synthesised 5′ to 3′. On one template the polymerase extends towards the moving fork. On the antiparallel template, it extends away from the fork, so new stretches of exposed template need new primers. Those fragments are processed and ligated.

Follow-up: Why does the leading strand need a primer at all?

Listen for: An existing 3′-OH is needed; continuous does not mean primer-free.

A polymerase still extends DNA but makes more errors. What might be defective?

Loss of proofreading is a candidate: a mismatch at the growing 3′ end would be less likely to be removed. But an error-prone polymerase could also be worse at selecting the correct incoming nucleotide. An error rate alone does not distinguish the two.

Follow-up: How would you separate those explanations in principle?

Listen for: Compare removal from a pre-existing mismatched end with discrimination between incoming nucleotides; these are different activities.

Why is one intermediate band after one replication round not decisive?

Both semiconservative and classic dispersive models can predict that band. The second round is more discriminating: distinct light and hybrid bands support semiconservative replication.

Follow-up: What would the conservative model predict at round one?

Listen for: Separate heavy and light bands, not a hybrid-only band.

Transcription and cis/trans regulation

Transcription regulation controls how readily a DNA template produces RNA. A promoter helps position transcription initiation, while regulatory sites recruit proteins that raise or lower initiation. A DNA site acts in cis because its effect is linked to that DNA molecule. A diffusible regulator can act in trans on compatible sites elsewhere in the same cell. Keeping the site, the regulator and the transcription machinery separate makes both operon regulation and genetic complementation easier to understand.

Orient transcription before interpreting regulation

RNA polymerase reads a template strand 3′→5′ and synthesises RNA 5′→3′. Across the transcribed segment, the RNA matches the coding strand except that U replaces T, before RNA processing changes the product. Gene direction determines which strand is the template; the upper strand in a drawing has no privileged role.

In bacteria, a sigma factor contributes to promoter recognition by RNA polymerase. An operator is a site at which a regulator binds. Operator and promoter can overlap, but the names describe different jobs: regulatory binding and positioning transcription initiation.

Read the lac operon as two separate inputs

The standard E. coli lac model combines relief of repression with positive activation.

  1. When inducer is scarce, LacI occupies operator DNA and represses transcription.
  2. Lactose supplies the precursor for allolactose, which reduces LacI binding to the operator. Repression is relieved.
  3. Under glucose limitation, cAMP–CAP supplies a positive input that stimulates transcription. CAP is the regulatory protein; cAMP is its ligand.
  4. Lactose availability together with low glucose therefore gives the canonical strong-expression state: repression is relieved and activation is supplied.

Removing a negative input and adding a positive input are different mechanisms. Expression is better understood as relative levels than as a perfectly digital switch. The diagram separates these regulatory effects rather than specifying the physical order of DNA sites.

Cis and trans describe where an effect can act

A changed operator affects the linked transcription unit. A mobile repressor can reach multiple compatible operators in the same cell. The same distinction applies to a eukaryotic enhancer and its transcription factor: the enhancer is cis-acting DNA, even when distant from the regulated gene, while the factor can act in trans.

Worked conceptual example: two reporters in one cell

Suppose copy A produces a green reporter and copy B a red reporter. Both share a diffusible functional repressor. Keep activation fixed, omit inducer and assume that reporter detection is otherwise comparable.

Regulatory state Copy A Copy B
A has an operator that cannot bind repressor; B is intact Escapes repression Remains repressed
Repressor activity is absent; both operators are intact Repression relieved Repression relieved

The first pattern follows the altered DNA site and is cis-acting. The second follows the missing diffusible activity and affects both copies. Supplying functional repressor can restore repression after a simple repressor loss, but cannot repair an operator that no longer binds it.

To connect this comparison specifically to transcription initiation, compare RNA production per DNA template. RNA persistence and reporter translation, maturation or degradation explain why fluorescence can lag behind or differ from the transcriptional response.

Oral practice

Why can a repressor mutation act in trans, whereas an operator mutation usually acts in cis?

The repressor is a diffusible product that can reach multiple compatible DNA copies. The operator is part of a particular DNA molecule and directly controls linked transcription. A second intact operator does not replace the defective site on the first copy.

Follow-up: Would adding functional repressor rescue both defects?

Listen for: It can rescue a simple repressor loss at an intact site, but not restore binding to a site that cannot bind.

With lactose present, why might lac expression still be lower when glucose is abundant?

Lactose-derived inducer relieves LacI repression. Low glucose supports activation through cAMP–CAP. With abundant glucose, that positive input is weaker, so relief of repression is not the same as maximal transcription.

Follow-up: Is CAP an activator or an inducer in this explanation?

Listen for: CAP is the regulatory protein; cAMP is its ligand, and allolactose acts on LacI.

Two cells give different reporter fluorescence. Does that prove different transcription rates?

No. Fluorescence reflects reporter amount and state. Differences in transcript stability, translation, reporter maturation, degradation, cell number or construct copy number can alter the endpoint. I would measure evidence closer to initiation and check that the copies and readout were comparable.

Follow-up: Would measuring steady-state reporter RNA settle the initiation claim?

Listen for: No; RNA abundance depends on synthesis and loss. A measure of nascent transcription is closer to the claim.

Chapter 16

RNA processing, translation and RNA abundance

A gene supplies a DNA template, but the mature RNA and protein are products of several further decisions. RNA processing defines transcript ends and exon connections; translation reads a coding sequence; RNA and protein turnover determine how much accumulates. These steps explain how unchanged DNA can produce different RNAs, and how unchanged RNA abundance can accompany a change in protein. The useful starting point is to identify exactly which transcript region or protein form a measurement detects.

Make a mature RNA

Most nuclear RNA polymerase II protein-coding transcripts acquire a 5′ cap, undergo splicing and receive a poly(A) tail after 3′ cleavage. Splicing removes selected introns and joins exons. An exon can contain an untranslated region, so exon and protein-coding sequence are not synonyms. Alternative splicing can change the protein sequence, the untranslated regions, or both.

Transcription and translation have separate start and end points. A transcription start site identifies the first nucleotide copied into RNA; a translation start identifies where the ribosome begins the coding sequence. Likewise, a stop codon ends translation, whereas cleavage and polyadenylation form the mature RNA end in the usual pathway. Bacterial mRNAs generally lack this canonical nuclear processing pathway, and bacterial transcription and translation can be coupled.

Translate codons into a peptide

  1. Translation initiation positions the ribosome at a start site. Many bacterial messages use a Shine–Dalgarno interaction; many eukaryotic messages use cap-dependent recruitment and scanning, with start-site context influencing recognition.
  2. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases attach amino acids to their corresponding tRNAs. During elongation, the ribosome selects an incoming charged tRNA at the A site.
  3. The growing peptide transfers from the P-site tRNA onto the amino acid carried at the A site. Translocation advances the ribosome and moves the tRNAs through it.
  4. A release factor recognises a stop codon and promotes peptide release. Throughout translation, the ribosome travels along mRNA 5′→3′ and the peptide grows from its N terminus towards its C terminus.

These are common initiation pathways, rather than rules covering every transcript. The direction of peptide growth and the distinction between charging, decoding and peptide transfer provide the mechanistic framework.

Worked conceptual example: the same total, different isoforms

A fictional gene produces E1–E2–E3 and E1–E3 transcripts. An RNA assay inside shared exon E3 detects both forms. An assay spanning the E1–E3 junction detects the skipped arrangement, while an E1–E2 junction assay detects inclusion of E2 in this example.

Suppose the shared-exon signal stays constant, the skipping-junction signal rises and the inclusion-junction signal falls. Together, these observations support redistribution between transcript forms rather than increased total gene RNA. Compare changes within each validated assay; raw Cq differences between different assays are not direct molecule counts. Sequencing junction reads provide complementary evidence about exon connectivity, although a junction shared by several transcripts does not identify an entire isoform.

The coding consequence depends on what was removed. Skipping 90 coding nucleotides preserves the downstream reading frame; skipping 91 changes it, assuming the same translation start and no other changes. Preserving the frame still allows a change in protein function.

Connect abundance to production and loss

Steady-state RNA reflects both synthesis and decay. Protein abundance adds translation and protein turnover to that balance. If RNA stays constant while a protein signal falls, measurements of protein production, disappearance and detected form help locate the changed step.

Oral practice

Can a single gene make two mature RNAs without changing its DNA?

Yes. The same primary transcript can be spliced in different ways, creating different exon junctions. These isoforms can differ in protein sequence or just in untranslated regions. The evidence should distinguish the relevant isoforms rather than simply measure a shared exon.

Follow-up: Would detecting one distinctive junction establish the entire isoform?

Listen for: Not necessarily; other distant junctions or transcript ends may differ.

An RT-qPCR signal is unchanged, but a protein band is half as strong. What is your first interpretation?

The measured RNA region appears unchanged and the measured protein signal is lower. Translation or protein turnover could explain it, as could a shift to a form the antibody detects less well. I would first check assay comparability and then ask whether protein production or loss changed.

Follow-up: What is one measurement that addresses protein loss rather than abundance alone?

Listen for: Follow target disappearance over time; distinguish a decay-rate measurement from another endpoint blot.

How do you tell a transcription start site from a translation start site?

The transcription start is the first nucleotide copied into RNA; the translation start is the codon at which the ribosome begins protein synthesis. An untranslated region can lie between them. The same distinction applies at the other end: a stop codon ends translation, not transcription.

Follow-up: With the same translation start, does skipping an internal segment of 90 coding nucleotides shift the downstream reading frame? What about 91?

Listen for: Assuming no other changes and that translation reaches the junction, removing 90 coding nucleotides preserves the downstream frame; removing 91 shifts it. A preserved frame does not imply preserved protein function.

Chapter 17

Protein targeting, trafficking and secretion

Protein secretion is a problem of targeting and membrane topology. A typical soluble mammalian secreted protein enters the endoplasmic reticulum while it is being translated, then travels through the secretory pathway inside membrane-enclosed compartments. Vesicle fusion opens that lumen to the extracellular space. The protein therefore crosses a membrane to enter the ER, but does not cross the plasma membrane again when released. Tracking which side of a membrane a domain occupies also predicts the orientation of surface proteins.

Follow a soluble cargo from synthesis to release

  1. Translation begins on a cytosolic ribosome. As an N-terminal ER signal sequence emerges, signal recognition particle, or SRP, recognises it and directs the ribosome–nascent-chain complex towards the ER.
  2. The ribosome engages the Sec61 translocon. Translation continues as the growing chain enters the ER lumen. In this common example, a cleavable signal peptide is removed.
  3. The protein folds and undergoes some modifications in the ER. Conventional cargo then travels through the Golgi, where further processing and sorting can occur.
  4. A cargo-containing vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane. Its lumen becomes continuous with the extracellular space, releasing the soluble protein.

The lumen remains separated from the cytosol during vesicular transport. Budding and fusion change which compartments are connected while preserving the membrane-facing orientation of cargo. This is why the ER lumen and the cell exterior are described as topologically equivalent.

Predict membrane-protein orientation

A targeting signal and a membrane anchor have different jobs. A cleaved signal peptide initiates entry and is removed. An internal signal anchor remains in the membrane; a stop-transfer segment can also become a membrane-spanning helix.

Consider the model protein with a cleaved N-terminal signal peptide followed by a stop-transfer segment. Its N-terminal domain enters the ER lumen, while its downstream C-terminal tail remains cytosolic. If conventional trafficking carries it to the cell surface, the N-terminal domain faces outside and the C-terminal tail still faces the cytosol. Different signal arrangements can produce different orientations, so trace the actual segments rather than assigning one topology to all single-pass proteins.

This prediction connects the pathway to accessibility measurements. An extracellular domain is accessible from outside an intact cell; a cytosolic tail is separated from that space by the plasma membrane.

Worked conceptual example: redistribution between compartments

Imagine a mammalian culture with unchanged target RNA, less target protein in the medium and more cell-associated target after accounting for viable cell number. The paired protein measurements suggest that the balance between intracellular retention and extracellular accumulation has changed. They make trafficking or release a useful hypothesis.

A follow-up should compare the intracellular and extracellular pools over time. Delayed appearance outside with accumulation inside fits impaired delivery or release. Normal appearance followed by faster disappearance outside instead points towards altered extracellular persistence. Viability and leakage measurements identify protein entering the medium through cell damage.

RNA adds context about transcript abundance, while the two protein compartments locate where the product accumulates. Protein production and degradation complete the interpretation because unchanged RNA can coexist with altered translation or turnover. The aim is to explain movement between pools, not simply to attach a secretion rate to one endpoint concentration. This pathway describes conventional secretion rather than every route by which proteins reach the extracellular space.

Oral practice

How does a soluble secreted protein get from a ribosome to the extracellular space?

Translation starts in the cytosol. An emerging ER signal recruits SRP; the ribosome docks and the chain enters the ER through a translocon. After folding and sorting in the secretory pathway, vesicle fusion releases the soluble cargo outside.

Follow-up: Does the protein cross the plasma membrane during release?

Listen for: No second crossing in this route; the vesicle lumen becomes continuous with the outside.

A single-pass reporter has its N terminus in the ER lumen and its C terminus in the cytosol. Which end will be exposed at the cell surface?

If the reporter follows the conventional vesicular route to the surface, the N terminus will face outside. The C terminus remains cytosolic because budding and fusion preserve membrane topology.

Follow-up: Why can an antibody against an extracellular tag bind intact cells, whereas a cytosolic tag is normally inaccessible?

Listen for: An intact plasma membrane separates the antibody from the cytosolic epitope; appropriate accessibility and expression controls matter.

Medium protein falls while cell-associated protein rises. Have you proved a secretion block?

No. Redistribution is compatible with a secretion defect, but extracellular loss and altered intracellular turnover can give similar endpoints. I would measure both compartments over time and account for viable cell number and leakage.

Follow-up: Is unchanged mRNA enough to rule out altered protein synthesis?

Listen for: No, translation can change without a change in RNA abundance.

Chapter 18

Enzyme kinetics and inhibition

An enzyme-rate curve connects molecular events with a measurable rate. In the simple scheme E + S ⇌ ES → E + P, substrate binding creates the enzyme–substrate complex and catalysis releases product. At low substrate, adding substrate strongly raises the rate; near saturation, most available enzyme is already engaged. Michaelis–Menten analysis describes this relationship under initial-rate and steady-state assumptions. It separates the limiting rate from the substrate concentration needed to reach half that rate.

Give the parameters their physical meaning

After the brief initial transient, ES forms and disappears at approximately equal rates, so its pool is nearly constant. This steady-state approximation gives

v₀ = Vmax[S]/(Km + [S])

At [S] = Km, v₀ is half of Vmax. Km therefore has concentration units, while v₀ and Vmax have units of product concentration per time. Vmax = kcat[E]total, where [E]total is the active enzyme concentration and kcat is turnover per active enzyme unit at saturation. More active enzyme raises Vmax even if each enzyme unit has unchanged catalytic behaviour.

Km combines binding and catalysis:

Km = (koff + kcat)/kon, whereas Kd = koff/kon.

Kd describes binding dissociation. Km approximates it when dissociation is much faster than turnover, the rapid-equilibrium limit. Otherwise, a changed Km need not imply a corresponding change in affinity. At low substrate, kcat/Km describes the slope after normalisation to active enzyme concentration.

Turn observations into a rate curve

  1. Identify an early interval after the brief initial transient in which product increases approximately linearly. Its slope estimates the initial rate, while substrate depletion and accumulated-product effects remain small.
  2. Compare initial rates across substrate concentrations at a defined active enzyme amount. The range should describe both the rising region and the approach to saturation.
  3. Fit untransformed rates to plausible models, then examine residuals and parameter uncertainty. Cooperativity, substrate inhibition and more complex reaction schemes may require a different model.
  4. Compare conditions using both the fitted parameters and assay checks. Readout interference, inactive enzyme or loss of initial-rate conditions can alter the apparent curve.

An endpoint absorbance measures accumulated signal, not automatically an initial rate. An ELISA calibration curve likewise answers a different question from enzyme saturation kinetics.

Compare ideal reversible inhibition patterns

Model Apparent Km Vmax
Competitive Increases Unchanged
Pure noncompetitive Unchanged Decreases
Uncompetitive Decreases Decreases by the same factor
Mixed Can increase or decrease Decreases

These patterns describe ideal models at fixed active enzyme concentration. Pure noncompetitive inhibition is a particular case, not a synonym for all inhibition that lowers Vmax. Use the full rate relationship to compare models rather than identifying a mechanism from one substrate concentration.

Worked conceptual example: a right-shifted curve

Suppose Vmax is 120 nM product/min and Km is 20 µM. At 20 µM substrate, the predicted rate is 120 × 20/(20 + 20) = 60 nM/min. At 60 µM, it is 120 × 60/(20 + 60) = 90 nM/min.

Now let a hypothetical competitive inhibitor increase apparent Km to 40 µM without changing Vmax. At 20 µM substrate, the rate becomes 120 × 20/(40 + 20) = 40 nM/min. More substrate is required to reach a given fraction of Vmax, but both curves approach the same limiting rate. That shared limit distinguishes the ideal competitive pattern from a simple loss of active enzyme.

Oral practice

What does Km mean, and when can you interpret it as a binding constant?

In the simple Michaelis–Menten model, Km is the substrate concentration at half Vmax. It includes both dissociation and catalysis. It approximates Kd when ES dissociates much faster than it turns over.

Follow-up: What units do Km and kcat have?

Listen for: Concentration and inverse time, respectively, not the same dimensions.

What pattern would support reversible competitive inhibition?

The initial-rate curve shifts right, so apparent Km rises, while the limiting rate stays the same at fixed active enzyme concentration. That supports competition under the model. I would compare fitted models over a substrate range rather than infer it from one point.

Follow-up: What if Vmax is lower but apparent Km is unchanged?

Listen for: The ideal pure noncompetitive pattern; loss of active enzyme can also lower Vmax, so the pattern is not proof of a binding mechanism.

Two preparations have the same Km but one has double the Vmax. Do the enzyme molecules work twice as fast?

Not necessarily. Vmax depends on both turnover and active enzyme amount. If active enzyme concentrations are equal and the model is appropriate, the preparation with double Vmax has double the turnover; otherwise enzyme amount remains an explanation.

Follow-up: Why use the early slope rather than product after a long incubation?

Listen for: Substrate depletion, product effects or enzyme instability can change the slope later.

Chapter 19

Plasmids: replication, maintenance and expression

A plasmid is a genetic replicon maintained separately from the chromosome in a suitable host. Many bacterial plasmids are circular DNA molecules, but shape is not their defining feature. Their replication still depends on cellular machinery and resources. Understanding a plasmid means separating three jobs: copying the DNA, passing copies to daughter cells and expressing particular sequences. A plasmid can serve as a vector for carrying genetic material, although plasmid and vector are not interchangeable terms.

Read a map by function

A plasmid map places different biological instructions on the same DNA molecule. In the fictional reporter shown, the origin concerns replication of the DNA, whereas the promoter concerns transcription of the reporter.

Map feature Main function
Origin of replication Participates in initiation of DNA copying
Promoter Helps position transcription initiation
Reporter coding region Specifies the protein sequence
Transcription terminator Is associated with ending transcription

A translation stop codon is different from a transcription terminator: the ribosome reads it to end protein synthesis. The promoter-to-reporter arrow shows transcription direction, not the direction in which the whole plasmid must replicate.

The backbone is the vector portion distinguished from the inserted DNA. These labels depend on how a construct is described or assembled. A map is an annotation of expected features and orientation; a sequence observation establishes which features are present in the sample.

Separate copying from inheritance

  1. Replication generates additional DNA copies. Copy number describes how many copies are present relative to a stated denominator, commonly cells or chromosome equivalents.
  2. Division distributes those copies between daughters. Some plasmids have partition systems that help this distribution, so maintenance includes inheritance as well as replication.
  3. Across the population, distinguish the copy-number distribution from the carrier fraction, the proportion of cells containing the plasmid.

Cell-to-cell transfer moves DNA between cells. Replication copies it within a host, inheritance passes it to descendants, and expression produces RNA or protein.

The denominator matters. A plasmid-to-chromosome ratio is not automatically copies per cell because chromosome equivalents per cell can vary with growth state. A stable population mean can also coexist with differences between individual cells.

Worked conceptual example: equal means, different populations

Population A contains ten cells, each carrying four plasmid copies. Population B contains five cells carrying eight copies and five carrying none. Both contain forty copies across ten cells, averaging four copies per cell. Their carrier fractions differ: 100% in A and 50% in B.

A bulk DNA measurement reports the same mean in both populations. A reliable single-cell measurement can distinguish uniform carriage from a mixture of carriers and non-carriers, provided low copy counts can be detected. The example explains why another population average cannot recover the distribution that averaging removed.

Connect DNA dosage with expression

More reporter DNA supplies more potential transcription templates. Reporter RNA then reflects transcription and RNA loss; protein reflects translation, maturation and degradation as well. Fluorescence is a downstream readout of this chain, rather than a direct measure of promoter activity per template.

Separate DNA, RNA and protein measurements therefore make different contributions. DNA establishes template amount and sequence, RNA describes transcript output, and protein describes the detected product. A functional readout connects that product to a phenotype. Shao and colleagues' separate single-cell measurements of plasmid DNA, RNA and protein illustrate this approach. Together, these layers help locate a change without treating every increase in reporter signal as a stronger promoter.

Oral practice

If you can detect plasmid DNA but no reporter protein, has the origin failed?

Not necessarily. The origin concerns replication, whereas reporter production depends on transcription, translation and protein detection. Detectable DNA does not tell me whether the reporter RNA or protein was produced. I would first separate the DNA, RNA and protein measurements.

Follow-up: Could a single snapshot of plasmid DNA prove that it is being replicated?

Listen for: No. Presence at one time point is not a measurement of new synthesis or inheritance.

Can two bacterial populations with the same mean plasmid copy number differ in plasmid retention?

Yes. A population in which each cell carries four copies and one in which half carry eight and half carry none both average four copies. The mean loses information about the fraction of carriers and the single-cell distribution.

Follow-up: What additional kind of information would distinguish your two examples?

Listen for: A carrier fraction or a reliable single-cell distribution, not merely another bulk average.

Two reporters differ in fluorescence. Have you measured promoter strength?

Fluorescence is a downstream output. Higher signal could reflect more DNA copies, changes in RNA loss, translation or protein state. I would compare evidence for DNA amount, reporter RNA and protein before assigning a change to transcription per template.

Follow-up: If steady-state RNA per DNA copy is higher, does that by itself prove faster transcription?

Listen for: No. Slower RNA loss could also increase steady-state abundance per template.

From an observed signal to a biological explanation

Biological measurements complement one another because they describe different parts of the same system. DNA identifies templates and regulatory sequences; RNA shows which transcripts accumulate; protein measurements describe products, their location and state; a phenotype reports the outcome for a cell or organism. Connecting these layers turns a collection of signals into an explanation. Agreement can support a proposed pathway, while a mismatch between layers helps locate the next informative measurement, especially when timing and biological units are matched.

Build a connected explanation

  1. State the biological change and comparison: for example, altered response relative to the same cell type's baseline. Identify the independent experimental units and the relevant time window.
  2. Map the proposed route through DNA, RNA, protein and phenotype. For each step, name a useful observable: template copy number, transcript or isoform abundance, protein amount or location, and a functional response.
  3. Compare compatible measurements. Matching samples, time points and denominators makes agreement or divergence between layers interpretable. An early RNA change and a later protein change may fit the expected order.
  4. Choose the follow-up that separates the remaining explanations. A synthesis measurement addresses production, a disappearance measurement addresses loss, and a localisation measurement addresses redistribution.

Worked conceptual example: RNA unchanged, response weaker

Imagine a fictional mammalian culture whose response to a harmless extracellular signal becomes weaker. The relevant DNA sequence and copy number are unchanged, total receptor RNA is unchanged, but less receptor is detected at the cell surface.

Each layer contributes: DNA establishes a comparable template, RNA shows that total transcript abundance need not change, surface protein places the difference near receptor availability, and the response measurement establishes the functional outcome. Comparing total cellular receptor with surface receptor over time would help distinguish changed production or turnover from redistribution. Following receptor availability and the response together tests whether their timing fits the proposed explanation.

Preserve the information the question needs

Sequence, structure and function require complementary observations. A sequence motif suggests a possible folded structure; a structural readout addresses folding in context; an expression comparison connects it to regulation. Joint measurements add another dimension. If sites A and B are each occupied on 50 of 100 DNA molecules, the occupied sets could be identical or disjoint. The same 50% occupancy at each site therefore permits either 50% or 0% co-occupancy. Measuring both sites on each molecule resolves this difference; direct contact between factors is a separate question.

Images describe cell states through features such as shape, texture and fluorescence distribution. To connect profiles with a treatment response, represent both conditions across independent batches and validate on held-out units relevant to the intended use. Cells from one treated well are multiple observations of that unit, not independent treatment assignments. If controls are imaged only on Monday and treated samples only on Tuesday, day and treatment are inseparable in those data.

Pool size and turnover also complement one another. At fixed volume, production and consumption of 2 units/min leave a metabolite pool unchanged, as do balanced rates of 10 units/min. A concentration time series describes net accumulation; tracking a marker through the system adds information about the underlying rates, interpreted with a model. The same balance applies to RNA and protein abundance.

Define the effect before explaining it

Suppose fictional yeast culture A has final densities of 100 units without mild salt stress and 80 with it; B has 60 and 55, respectively. A ends higher, but its absolute decrease is 20 units versus 5 for B. The difference in changes is −15 units. Choose an absolute or proportional comparison to match the question, then use growth, viability or other functional measurements to explain that outcome.

Oral practice

Two DNA sites are each occupied on half the molecules. Must the sites be co-occupied?

No. The same marginal occupancies can come from the same molecules or disjoint sets. A joint measurement per molecule can tell those cases apart.

Follow-up: Would observing co-occupancy prove direct contact between the proteins?

Listen for: No. Shared context or another factor could explain it.

A predictive model separates treated and control cells, but treatment is confounded with imaging day. Is the test convincing?

Not for a treatment effect. Day and treatment cannot be distinguished, and random cell splits can share the same wells and run effects. I would want both groups on multiple days and a test holding out the relevant independent units.

Follow-up: If the treatment was assigned to wells, are hundreds of cells hundreds of independent treatments?

Listen for: No. Identify the unit of assignment and any dependence between wells, for example common donors.

An unchanged metabolite concentration is observed over time. Must both production and consumption be unchanged?

No. An unchanged pool means inputs and outputs balance under the model, not that their individual rates are unchanged. Both can increase by the same amount.

Follow-up: Does a higher final cell density establish better protection under stress?

Listen for: Not without baselines and a stated comparison. Density, viability and a mechanistic claim are different outcomes.

Enhancers and promoters: from DNA folding to joint occupancy

A promoter provides a local site for transcription initiation, while an enhancer supplies regulatory input to a promoter.[3][8] Understanding their relationship means connecting the DNA sequence with the molecular state actually present and the transcriptional output. This lesson develops that connection through a new probability example: apparent cooperation between two DNA-binding factors can emerge from a mixture of accessible and inaccessible molecules. All numerical data below are hypothetical and describe a harmless eukaryotic model.

Promoter and enhancer describe different jobs

At a eukaryotic promoter, transcription machinery assembles to begin RNA synthesis at a local start site.[3] Enhancer activity describes stimulation of initiation at a promoter, potentially at a distance.[8] DNA folding can bring regulatory regions closer in three-dimensional space.[3] These roles need not identify mutually exclusive DNA segments: a primary comparison in mouse neurons found sequences with both activities.[8] Local initiation and stimulation of another promoter therefore require distinct functional comparisons.[8]

A sequence motif is a possibility; folding is a molecular state

A motif is a recognisable sequence pattern. Some guanine-rich sequences can form G-quadruplexes, or G4s: four guanines make a planar quartet, and stacked quartets create a folded structure.[1] This local secondary structure is distinct from long-range chromatin folding that brings distant regions together.[1][3] A sequence prediction identifies folding potential; a structure-sensitive observation addresses the state present in context.[1] A cell-based globin-enhancer study connected folded G4s with enhancer-promoter interactions and gene expression at particular enhancers.[1] Keep the chain clear: sequence supplies possibilities, a physical measurement addresses structure, and a functional comparison relates the observed state to transcription.

Why chromatin belongs in the explanation

Chromatin packages DNA with proteins, including histones, so regulatory sites can differ in their accessibility to binding factors.[6] The EMBL account of single-molecule footprinting explains how observations on individual molecules support inference of factor co-occupancy that bulk averages cannot reconstruct; it also describes a relationship between co-occupancy and chromatin packaging.[6] This provides a concrete reason to include local state in our model. In the fictional example below, we ask whether a shared accessible state could generate an association between factors A and B even if neither factor directly recruits the other.

Worked example: compare the joint fraction with independence

Imagine 1,200 DNA molecules sampled at the same fictional regulatory region. Factor A occupies 480 molecules, factor B occupies 360, and both occupy 216. The marginal fractions are 40% and 30%; the joint fraction is 18%. If the two occupancy events were independent across the whole sample, their joint fraction would be 0.40 × 0.30 = 0.12, corresponding to 144 molecules. The observed 216 exceeds that prediction. This establishes a positive association in these invented observations and makes an explicit independence model testable. It does not yet specify the mechanism behind the association.

A shared-state model reproduces the same observations

Suppose 800 of the 1,200 molecules are accessible, while 400 are inaccessible to both factors. Within the accessible subgroup, let A bind independently with probability 0.60 and B with probability 0.45. The model predicts 800 × 0.60 = 480 A-bound molecules, 800 × 0.45 = 360 B-bound molecules, and 800 × 0.60 × 0.45 = 216 jointly occupied molecules. Those expected counts match all three observations. Whole-sample association therefore coexists with independence within the accessible subgroup. Pooling two molecular states created the association. This is our mathematical teaching model, not a claim that it explains the published study. Its purpose is to demonstrate a specific alternative to direct recruitment.

Use a conditional comparison to separate explanations

Our shared-state-only model predicts 27% joint occupancy within accessible molecules, because 0.60 × 0.45 = 0.27. There is no excess over independence within that subgroup, although there is an excess in the pooled sample. A conceptual follow-up would compare the joint fraction with the independence prediction within reliably identified, comparable accessibility states. A remaining association would challenge this simple mixture model and motivate alternatives such as an unmeasured cofactor or interdependent binding. Finally, connect any binding model to promoter output with a separate transcriptional comparison. Co-occupancy is a relationship between binding events; enhancer activity is a relationship to transcription.[6][8]

Oral practice

Does 18% co-occupancy prove that factor A directly recruits factor B?

No. With 40% A occupancy and 30% B occupancy, whole-sample independence predicts 12%. Shared accessibility can produce 18% without direct recruitment in our hypothetical model.

Follow-up: What happens to the association within the accessible subgroup in our model?

Listen for: It disappears: 0.60 × 0.45 = 0.27, matching that subgroup's joint occupancy. State which population supplies the denominator.

If a DNA region initiates RNA, must it be exclusively a promoter?

No. Local RNA initiation demonstrates promoter activity, whereas enhancer activity concerns stimulation of transcription at a promoter. The same DNA segment can show both activities, as demonstrated in a functional comparison in mouse neurons.[8]

Follow-up: Which comparison would address enhancer activity rather than local initiation?

Listen for: A comparison of transcription from the target promoter with the regulatory input varied conceptually; distinguish that output from RNA initiated at the tested region.[8]

Why is a predicted G4 motif different from a measured folded G4?

A motif identifies sequence-based folding potential. A structure-sensitive measurement addresses a physical state in the sampled context. Connecting that state with enhancer function requires a transcriptional comparison, not merely a motif match.[1]

Follow-up: How would you distinguish shape-dependent recognition from recognition of particular base identities conceptually?

Listen for: Compare observations that separate primary sequence identity from measured shape in a matched context, then relate binding and transcription to the appropriate property. No sequence specification or engineering plan is needed.
