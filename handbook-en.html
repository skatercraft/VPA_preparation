Chapter 01

Sanger sequencing and trace interpretation

The question this method answers: What is the sequence of the dominant template represented in this read, and how confidently can I call the bases in the region I care about?

A primer provides a starting point for DNA polymerase. Ordinary deoxynucleotides leave a 3′ hydroxyl group that supports continued extension. A dideoxynucleotide (ddNTP) lacks that group, so no further nucleotide can be attached to that strand. Different copies stop at different positions. The fluorescent tag identifies the terminating base; the tag is not what stops the chain. The result is a set of fragments sharing one starting point but ending at successive positions.

From fluorescence to a sequence

Capillary electrophoresis separates the products by length. As fragments reach the detector, fluorescence is recorded in channels associated with the four bases. The base caller infers the sequence from the order and quality of the peaks. The called sequence describes the newly synthesised strand in the 5′→3′ direction. A read from the opposite orientation must be compared in the equivalent orientation, typically by taking its reverse complement. The short segment shown here is fictional and not a reference for dye colours or peak heights.

The chromatogram is evidence, not just a decorative version of the base calls. A clean but unexpected peak at a well-resolved position supports a discrepancy with the expected sequence. It does not identify the cause of the discrepancy. A single ambiguous position can reflect noise or multiple templates; a long stretch of overlapping, shifted peaks can be consistent with a mixture of templates differing by an insertion or deletion. A clean trace does not exclude a sufficiently rare variant, verify a second junction outside the read, or establish the sequence of every molecule in the tube.

Worked case. A construct gives an expected-size band but a clear base substitution in the read. These observations are compatible. The band reports approximate size; replacing one base with another need not change that size. Ask whether the position is well resolved, whether the comparison uses the right sequence and orientation, and whether independent evidence supports a discrepancy. Do not call the clone entirely correct or infer why the difference arose.

Panel question and short answer

Question: How does Sanger sequencing work, and what can you infer from a clean trace?

A ddNTP stops extension because it lacks a 3′ hydroxyl. The instrument separates fragments by length and reads the dye on each terminal base. A clean trace supports the dominant sequence in the high-quality region. I would still compare it with the expected sequence.

Chapter 02

Gibson assembly and other assembly logic

The question: What makes the intended pieces meet in the intended order, and what remains to be verified afterwards?

In Gibson assembly, overlaps encode which fragments are neighbours. A 5′ exonuclease exposes complementary single-stranded regions. Matching regions can anneal. DNA polymerase fills gaps where bases are missing, and DNA ligase seals remaining nicks in the backbone. A gap is a stretch of missing nucleotides; a nick is a break between adjacent nucleotides in one strand. In an interview, explaining that distinction shows more understanding than listing the three enzymes alone.

Compare the source of specificity

  • Restriction and ligation: compatible DNA ends created by cleavage permit joining. Two different, non-interchangeable ends can constrain orientation; identical compatible ends generally do not enforce it on their own.
  • Gibson: overlapping complementary regions guide joining. The junction need not contain a particular restriction recognition site. A junction described as scarless does not have to acquire extra bases merely because the fragments were joined. It is not a certificate of sequence accuracy.
  • Golden Gate: a type IIS enzyme recognises one sequence and cuts outside it. The resulting compatible overhangs can specify the order and orientation of parts. The recognition sequence and the joining interface are distinct ideas.

A useful comparison starts with the design question, not a claim that one method is universally best. Is the main constraint the sequence of the junction, reuse of a standard set of pieces, or working with several parts? If an assembly is intended to create a reporting construct, its sequence and its readout are still separate claims. An observed output does not automatically tell you that all joints match the intended map.

Panel question and short answer

Question: What is the difference between Gibson and Golden Gate?

Gibson uses sequence overlaps. An exonuclease exposes them, polymerase fills gaps, and ligase seals nicks. In Golden Gate, type IIS enzymes cut outside their recognition sites and the overhangs define which pieces join.

Read further: Gibson et al.: overlapping DNA fragments · Engler et al.: type IIS assembly · Addgene: comparison of cloning techniques.

Chapter 03

PCR, gels and clone verification

The difference between a signal and an identification

A conventional PCR readout depends on whether DNA compatible with the assay is amplified. The position of a band on a gel gives an approximate size relative to a marker. It is not a base-by-base identification. A product of a plausible size can be non-specific; an absent band can mean either absence of the target or failure to detect it. A junction PCR is informative about the particular boundary it interrogates, to the extent that the assay distinguishes it from other possibilities. Detecting one boundary does not establish the rest of the construct.

An evidence map, not a ladder

Start with the claim and select a measurement that actually bears on it. A screen can narrow the set of candidates. Sequence alignment can assess the covered region and report discrepancies or gaps. Evidence for expression may come from an appropriate RNA or protein readout. A functional assay addresses a biological outcome, but a positive outcome alone does not identify which molecular change caused it. These are complementary measurements, not four levels where the last automatically proves the first three.

Worked case. A hypothetical gel shows the expected-size product for one junction and no product for a second. If the second assay also fails on the known positive control, that negative sample result is not informative. If the positive control performs as expected, the sample remains a candidate with incomplete evidence, not a verified full-length construct. A clean sequence read covering the first junction only still leaves the second unresolved.

Panel question and short answer

Question: Does a correctly sized band confirm the clone?

It supports a candidate with the expected product size, provided the assay and its controls are interpretable. It does not establish the exact sequence, structure elsewhere, or function.

Read further: Addgene: what colony PCR can screen for · Addgene: sequence interpretation.

Chapter 04

qPCR, RT-qPCR and normalisation

What does Cq mean?

In qPCR, an instrument follows fluorescence associated with amplification. The quantification cycle (Cq) is a value estimated on the cycle scale from that curve. In a threshold-based analysis, it is the cycle at which the curve crosses the threshold; not every instrument or algorithm uses that definition. Under comparable analysis and measurement conditions, an earlier Cq for the same assay is consistent with more initial target. Efficiency and inhibition can also shift curves. Endpoint yield and starting quantity are not interchangeable, and comparing uncalibrated Cq values across unrelated assays can be misleading.

RT-qPCR measures RNA indirectly via reverse transcription to cDNA. A no-template control asks whether the reaction can yield signal without added target material. A no-RT control instead asks whether a signal in an RNA workflow might arise from DNA rather than RNA-derived cDNA. Neither substitutes for checking the stability of reference targets. A single melting peak can be a useful check in appropriate assays but does not identify the sequence.

Two differences with two separate jobs

With ΔCq = Cq(target) − Cq(reference), the first difference uses an internal reference. ΔΔCq = ΔCq(test) − ΔCq(calibrator) compares the normalised measurements across conditions. Under the ideal assumptions behind the simple comparative model, including near doubling and comparable efficiencies, relative quantity is 2 raised to the power −ΔΔCq. If those assumptions do not hold, the simple result is not a universal correction; the analysis must account for efficiency and be appropriate to the question. MIQE concerns the quality and reporting of qPCR, not a command to use one calculation.

Case 1. The control has ΔCq = 5 and the test has ΔCq = 3. ΔΔCq = −2, corresponding to about four times as much relative target under the simple model. That is a normalised sample-level comparison, not a count of transcripts per cell.

Case 2. The target Cq is unchanged, but the reference Cq drops by one cycle in the test. ΔCq then rises by one. The simple calculation would report a halved target-to-reference ratio even though the target Cq did not move. A changed ratio is real as a description of those inputs; the claim that the target changed requires a defensible reference. Technical wells estimate repeatability, not additional biological sample size.

Panel question and short answer

Question: What does a lower Cq tell you?

Under comparable conditions for the same assay, it is consistent with more starting target. I would also check efficiency, inhibition and the analysis method. A comparison between groups needs an appropriate reference or calibration.

Read further: Bustin et al.: MIQE 2.0 · Livak and Schmittgen: comparative method · Ruijter et al.: baseline and amplification efficiency.

Chapter 05

FISH and spatial evidence

Fluorescence in situ hybridisation uses a labelled probe that recognises a complementary DNA or RNA region in a preserved sample. It retains spatial information: instead of only asking how much signal is associated with a pooled sample, you can ask where detectable target is distributed across cells or compartments. The method measures a signal produced by the probe-target system, not simply a direct count of active cells. Accessibility, background and the imaging conditions can affect signal intensity.

Read absence and overlap cautiously

A signal at the expected location with interpretable controls supports localisation. An apparently negative specimen could also contain inaccessible target, weak signal below detection, or an unsuitable measurement. A positive control supports the ability to detect a target; a background or specificity control addresses a different concern. In multicolour imaging, apparent overlap can reflect limited optical resolution, signal spread, or features at different depths projected onto a single image. Co-localisation can support proximity at the scale of the image. It is not proof of physical binding.

Worked case. Two fictional samples give similar pooled abundance, but in one, signal is distributed across many cells, whereas in the other, a small number of cells account for much of it. Those findings are compatible. Inversely, a change in average FISH intensity need not imply corresponding per-cell changes in target copy number. State which observable was measured in each assay before comparing their results.

Panel question and short answer

Question: Why use FISH if you already have qPCR?

qPCR estimates the abundance of a selected target across a sample. FISH tells me where the detectable target is. Together they can help distinguish a change in abundance from a change in distribution.

Read further: Amann et al.: oligonucleotide probes in intact cells · Fuchs et al.: target accessibility.

Chapter 06

CRISPR-Cas and levels of evidence

Natural system versus research tool

CRISPR-Cas is a family of microbial adaptive immune systems, not a synonym for the Cas9 protein. In the Cas9 example, RNA contributes sequence specificity and a compatible protospacer-adjacent motif (PAM) is part of target recognition. Different Cas systems do not all behave the same way. As a research-tool concept, recruitment of the nuclease, cleavage, stable change at a locus, and the effects of that change are distinct events. Cleavage alone is not an observation of a stable genotype.

What did the measurement actually test?

Detecting Cas9 protein establishes detection of the tool, not a change to the target DNA. A population-average sequence read can miss a mixture or variants outside its coverage. A detected sequence change is not, by itself, proof of loss of function. A knockout is genetic inactivation of a gene; a knockdown reduces expression or gene product without necessarily genetically inactivating the locus. Changes in RNA, protein, activity or phenotype require evidence appropriate to each. If you maintained a pre-existing inducible cell line, that is not the same contribution as constructing it, demonstrating editing, or validating a phenotype.

Panel question and short answer

Question: If you detect Cas9, can you conclude the gene was edited?

No. That shows the tool is present. I would separate evidence that the target sequence changed from evidence that the change altered gene function.

Read further: Jinek et al.: RNA-guided cleavage by Cas9.

Chapter 07

ELISA, cytokines and detection

In a sandwich ELISA, a capture antibody retains the analyte and a second antibody detects a different accessible feature of the captured analyte. The immunoassay generates a signal related to the amount of analyte; the relationship is determined by its calibration curve in the working range, not by an assumption of a linear response at all concentrations. The composition of the sample, interfering substances, background and how well an antibody recognises the intended analyte can affect estimates. A value below the lower quantification limit is not proof of zero; an assay may detect a signal without being able to quantify it reliably.

Concentration in medium versus production by a cell

At one time point, the concentration of detectable cytokine outside the cells reflects multiple processes. These include production, secretion, uptake, loss or breakdown, the number of contributing cells and, where relevant, the medium volume. A measured concentration is not an activity assay. The accessible part of a cytokine measured by the immunoassay need not track biological activity one-to-one.

Worked case. Group A has more detectable cytokine than control at 24 hours, and group B does not have a statistically significant difference from control. Say exactly which comparison supports your claim. Do not infer A differs from B without a direct contrast. Do not infer a higher production rate if you measured the concentration in the medium. An independent readout of cell abundance or an appropriate measure of synthesis or loss could make alternative explanations easier to evaluate.

Panel question and short answer

Question: There is more cytokine in the supernatant. Did the cells produce more?

We found more detectable cytokine in the medium at that time point. Increased production is one possibility, but secretion, loss and the number of contributing cells can also affect that measurement.

First name what is attached to the plate, then where the reporter is

An antigen-down assay has antigen immobilised at the surface. In direct detection, the antigen-binding antibody itself carries the label. In indirect detection, a labelled secondary recognises the primary antibody. In a sandwich assay, the capture antibody is immobilised, and the analytical target is bound between that antibody and an antigen-specific detector. That detector may itself carry HRP (direct detection) or may be detected by a separate labelled reagent, such as streptavidin–HRP binding biotin on the detector. The term direct ELISA often names antigen-down with a labelled primary, so if someone calls a kit ‘direct’, describe the actual binding layers rather than relying on the label. HRP tells you the reporter chemistry, not how the target is immobilised.

Competition reverses the readout in the illustrated format

A labelled analogue and sample analyte compete for a limited number of binding sites. As unlabelled analyte increases, less labelled analogue binds, so the measured signal decreases in this example. A different assay geometry may measure a different species; identify exactly what generates the signal before predicting the curve direction. Competition can be useful when there is only one accessible site for an antibody, whereas a two-antibody sandwich needs both antibodies to bind at the same time.

The enzyme amplifies the binding signal

Horseradish peroxidase (HRP) can use hydrogen peroxide to oxidise 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB). A common colourimetric system gives blue oxidised TMB, converting to yellow upon acid stopping, with a typical absorbance readout near 450 nm. This is one HRP readout, not a universal property of all peroxidase substrates. Absorbance reports the coloured product, not antibody binding or analyte concentration directly. Time, background and sample composition can affect the readout.

A high signal is not always a high-confidence number

Use the supported calibration range rather than extrapolating from a plateau. A value below the lower limit of quantification can be detectable yet too uncertain to quantify. A very high sample can give low signal in some sandwich assays due to a high-dose hook effect; that is distinct from a plateau, where signal levels off. Matrix effects can also make measured concentration depend on the sample’s composition. An in-range dilution that gives consistent back-calculated concentrations supports dilutional agreement. Known-analyte spike recovery asks a different question: whether added analyte is recovered as expected in that sample matrix. Neither alone proves perfect specificity or identical behaviour of the endogenous analyte and reference standard.

Example. Two in-range samples estimated at 30 and 45 pg/mL differ by 15 pg/mL in detectable analyte under the stated calibration. If the higher signal lies above the quantification range, do not assign 45 pg/mL by extrapolation. A valid time-matched concentration difference does not, by itself, tell you whether cells made more protein, released more, or lost less from the medium. Compare the result with the reasoning in protein secretion and enzyme kinetics.

Your assay uses HRP. How would you tell direct antigen-down detection from a sandwich with an HRP-labelled detector?

I would identify what is immobilised. In direct antigen-down detection, antigen is on the plate and an HRP-labelled primary binds it. In a sandwich, the plate has a capture antibody, and a second antigen-specific antibody can carry HRP directly. Both can use the same reporter.

Follow-up: What changes if the detector carries biotin instead of HRP?

Listen for: Streptavidin–HRP binds biotin on the detector; the capture format can remain a sandwich.

Two samples have nearly identical absorbance near the upper plateau. Are they equally concentrated?

Not necessarily. The response changes little with concentration near saturation, so the readout may not resolve a difference. I would seek measurements in the supported range and check agreement after accounting for dilution and matrix effects.

Follow-up: How is the high-dose hook effect different from saturation?

Listen for: In some sandwich formats extreme analyte can lower, not merely flatten, signal; low signal is not always low analyte.

In a competitive assay, why can a darker result mean less sample target?

In the labelled-tracer design, sample target competes with labelled tracer for binding sites. The reporter measures bound tracer, not unlabelled sample target. If more tracer remains bound, there may have been less sample target under the assay’s calibrated conditions.

Follow-up: Would every assay called competitive necessarily show the same curve direction?

Listen for: Identify the detected species and assay geometry; explain the direction for that design.

Chapter 08

Flow cytometry and Annexin V/PI

What is the denominator of your percentage?

A flow cytometer records light scatter and fluorescence associated with each detected event. An event is not necessarily a single cell: debris and coincident or aggregated cells can enter the stream. A gating hierarchy determines which events are included before a percentage is calculated. Forward and side scatter are useful empirical descriptors, not direct, universal measurements of cell diameter and granularity. The percentage of marker-positive events in a chosen gate does not report the absolute number of such cells unless there is an independent way to estimate counts.

Three controls, three purposes

An unstained sample shows background and autofluorescence. Single-stain controls help estimate spectral spillover for compensation in conventional multicolour cytometry. Fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls contain the rest of the panel but omit the marker of interest, helping set the boundary in the presence of the other markers and their spread. An FMO is not a substitute for a single-stain compensation control. The precise controls you need depend on the measurement and panel.

Annexin V binds exposed phosphatidylserine; PI uptake indicates a compromised membrane under the conditions of this assay. Annexin V-positive/PI-negative events can be consistent with early apoptosis; doubly positive events can fit later apoptosis or other forms of membrane damage. The four quadrants are marker-defined categories, not diagnoses. Additional evidence is needed to distinguish mechanisms. Time-resolved data help describe the sequence of marker changes but do not by themselves establish the mechanism.

Panel question and short answer

Question: Does double positivity prove apoptosis?

No. It shows exposed phosphatidylserine and a compromised membrane in those events. That combination can be consistent with late apoptosis, but I would not assign the mechanism from the two markers alone.

Chapter 09

Western blot and total protein

From a band to a defensible comparison

In a typical denaturing SDS-PAGE system, migration is mainly associated with molecular size. After separation and transfer, the antibody-based signal indicates recognised material on the membrane. A band near the expected apparent molecular mass is supportive but not proof of identity. Antibody cross-reactivity, alternative protein forms and sample degradation can complicate interpretation. Calling a band stronger is not equivalent to estimating how much more protein is present. Saturation flattens the relationship between amount and signal, and the target and loading reference can saturate at different amounts.

What does Bradford add?

Bradford measures total protein through a dye-binding response, not the abundance of the target protein. Different protein compositions and sample components can affect the relationship between total protein and signal. A measurement before loading does not verify that transfer worked, that detection is linear, or that the loading-control protein is stable. Total protein staining and a stable loading control are possible normalisation references; a denominator has to be justified. If the normalisation reference also changes biologically, the ratio may change even when target abundance does not. An abundance readout is not an activity readout.

Panel question and short answer

Question: What does a darker band tell you?

It can support more detectable target if the band is specific, the signal is in its quantitative range, and the comparison is appropriately normalised. The band alone does not measure activity.

Chapter 10

Experimental design and statistical contrasts

Identify the unit, the comparison and the uncertainty

The experimental unit is set by what was independently assigned to a condition or independently sampled for the question of interest. Many events from one cytometry file, wells made from one lysate, or images nested within one donor do not automatically provide that many independent biological observations. A paired design can compare conditions within each donor or sample, preserving the pairing in the analysis. A plot of all measurements may be useful, but treating nested measurements as independent can make uncertainty look unjustifiably small.

A positive control shows whether the study can detect an expected signal under its conditions. A negative control helps evaluate background or specificity. A vehicle control addresses effects of the vehicle. Blocking, randomisation and blinding address different sources of bias or variation. More replicates cannot retrospectively separate two factors that were completely confounded. If every control is in batch 1 and every test in batch 2, the biological comparison and the batch comparison coincide.

Worked case. A differs from control but B does not. Those outcomes result from two different comparisons; the contrast A minus B still needs its own estimate and uncertainty. Similarly, if A goes from 10 to 16 and B from 10 to 14, the observed difference in changes is 2, but the means alone give no measure of its uncertainty. If asking whether groups respond differently over time, make the difference between their changes the target. Report the effect size, uncertainty and design along with a p-value. A non-significant result alone does not establish equivalence.

Panel question and short answer

Question: I tested A against control and B against control. Can I claim A differs from B?

No. I need to estimate A versus B directly, account for the experimental design, and report the size and uncertainty of the difference.

Read further: Lazic et al.: experimental units and pseudoreplication · Gelman and Stern: the difference between significant and not significant.

Chapter 11

Bioinformatics and bulk RNA sequencing

Keep the evidence chain visible

A FASTQ record contains an observed read and base-quality scores. Base-call confidence differs from mapping confidence, which concerns the assignment of a read to a reference location. Alignment or transcript quantification, annotation and aggregation turn reads into a measurement of genes or transcripts. A count matrix without sample metadata does not define a biological comparison. Relevant details include sample provenance, batch, condition and pairing. RNA abundance, variants in DNA and protein activity are different questions even if some file formats or workflows are shared.

Normalisation, PCA and the contrast

Normalisation methods address technical differences such as library scale or composition under stated assumptions. They do not make all biological or technical variation disappear. PCA on suitable transformed values summarises major sources of sample variation. If samples separate by condition, check whether batch, donor or cell composition matches that split. If condition and batch are completely confounded, the data alone cannot identify their separate effects. Transformed expression for visualisation is not automatically valid input for a count-based model.

Worked case. A gene has an estimated log₂ fold change of +1 for test versus control. The estimated ratio is two to one in that direction; it is not a statement about measurement precision or the probability of being a genuine discovery. An FDR threshold concerns the expected proportion of false discoveries among discoveries under the procedure's assumptions, not the probability that a particular gene is a false hit. Bulk RNA-seq averages across a mixture: an apparent upregulation could reflect a changed cell mixture rather than more RNA of that gene within each cell type.

Make your contribution concrete

Explain what data came in, what decision you made about their quality or comparison, and what output someone else could reproduce. Keep a link between groups in the sample table, clarity on which direction a comparison is written, and a record of relevant reference, annotation and analysis choices. Be clear whether an example came from work, a course, or a project you are proposing to do.

Panel question and short answer

Question: The PCA separates the two conditions. What would you check?

I’d check whether condition overlaps with batch or sample composition and examine the sample-level metadata. PCA describes variation; it does not tell me what caused it.

Chapter 12

Microbiome data, bacteriophages and AMR

Microbiome: know the denominator

A relative abundance is a share of a total. A taxon can lose share without losing absolute abundance if the total increases. A library's read depth does not independently establish microbial load in the original sample. Extraction, library construction, classification and reference representation can also affect how taxa are represented. Amplicon and shotgun surveys give different kinds of evidence; taxonomic membership is not itself proof of which biological function is active or of a causal host-microbe relationship.

Phages: separate states and methods of inference

Lytic infection produces progeny and ultimately leads to lysis. Temperate phages can also establish a state where their genome persists in a bacterium as a prophage, often integrated but sometimes maintained outside the chromosome. A prophage-like sequence by itself does not establish that the element is complete or producing infectious progeny. Computational host prediction proposes an association at a given taxonomic resolution; a prediction at genus level does not demonstrate productive infection of a specified strain. Absence from a catalogue or database can mean incomplete sampling. Look for a held-out evaluation and ask how the task defined a correct prediction.

Resistance is not the only reason cells survive

Resistance is associated with growth at higher antibiotic concentrations, reflected in a shift in MIC under comparable conditions. Clinical resistance categories, where applicable, are a separate interpretation using appropriate criteria. Tolerance concerns slower killing without a corresponding increase in MIC. Persistence describes heterogeneous tolerance in which a subpopulation survives bactericidal exposure longer without increased resistance. The mere observation of a surviving subpopulation does not distinguish persistence from heteroresistance, which involves a subpopulation with higher resistance. State whether the underlying readout measured growth inhibition or time-dependent survival.

Panel question and short answer

Question: A bacterium was predicted to be a host. What does that mean?

The prediction supports a possible association at the reported taxonomic level. It does not demonstrate that a particular strain supports productive infection.

Chapter 13

Presenting your own research

Keep the short answer short

Start with the biological question, not a list of techniques. Define the experimental model in terms the panel can follow. Say what you personally did, report one result with its comparator, then identify one thing the result cannot establish. If the work is unfinished, describe a decision you can defend and the evidence behind it; do not imply that a hoped-for endpoint was achieved. If asked what to do differently, name a weakness that changed the inference and connect the improvement to that weakness.

Train three versions of the same example

In 20 seconds, name the question, your part and the result. In about one minute, add the key control and competing interpretation. When challenged, answer the exact question first: for example, specify the denominator of a percentage or give the actual statistical comparison. Then explain which observation would make you revise your interpretation. Pick one figure you can describe aloud, including axes, units, independent observations and uncertainty.

A good reason to join a PhD programme is specific to the work and training you want. Say what kind of analysis you would like to own and what kind of support would help you do it independently. If a topic is new to you, state your transferable experience and the question you want to learn to address rather than implying hands-on experience you do not have. Follow the timing and format in your invitation rather than assuming every interview round is identical.

Panel question and short answer

Question: Tell us about a result you can defend.

We asked whether… My part was… Compared with…, I observed… That supports…, but this measurement alone does not distinguish…

Chapter 14

DNA replication and repair

What to draw on a whiteboard

Start with an antiparallel duplex and mark all four ends. At a fork moving right, a template oriented 3′→5′ from left to right can be copied continuously towards the fork. The opposite template is copied away from the fork in short Okazaki fragments. The two new strands are both made 5′→3′. DNA polymerase adds an incoming nucleotide to the growing strand’s 3′-OH; the lagging strand is discontinuous because of template orientation, not because a different synthesis chemistry runs backwards.

What each component changes

Helicase separates the paired strands. Topoisomerases address torsional stress ahead of the fork. Single-strand-binding proteins protect exposed templates. Primase makes primers that supply a starting 3′ end; the leading strand also needs a start, while each new lagging-strand fragment needs its own start. A sliding clamp helps keep the polymerase associated with DNA. Primer removal and replacement leave backbone discontinuities; ligase seals nicks. A nick is a missing backbone bond, not a stretch of missing nucleotides. The exact proteins responsible for processing primers differ between bacteria and eukaryotes.

Proofreading, repair and an informative result

A replicative polymerase’s 3′→5′ exonuclease activity can remove a misincorporated base from the growing 3′ end, after which synthesis can continue 5′→3′. Mismatch repair is a subsequent process that corrects some errors that escaped proofreading. Base-excision repair typically begins by removing a chemically altered base; nucleotide-excision repair can remove a short segment around a bulky lesion. A mismatch is not necessarily a fixed mutation: subsequent copying can turn the mismatch into a stable sequence change in one lineage if it escapes correction.

Classic evidence. In the idealised Meselson–Stahl model, after one round of copying heavy duplexes in a light environment, semiconservative replication gives hybrid duplexes. A conservative model would give separate heavy and light duplexes. A dispersive model could also give one intermediate band after the first round. After a second round, distinct hybrid and light bands distinguish semiconservative replication from the classic dispersive prediction of a single band shifting towards light density. Density reports the distribution of label, not the identity of a particular fork enzyme.

Oral practice

Why can leading-strand synthesis be continuous while the lagging strand is made in fragments?

Both DNA strands are synthesised 5′ to 3′. On one template the polymerase extends towards the moving fork. On the antiparallel template, it extends away from the fork, so new stretches of exposed template need new primers. Those fragments are processed and ligated.

Follow-up: Why does the leading strand need a primer at all?

Listen for: An existing 3′-OH is needed; continuous does not mean primer-free.

A polymerase still extends DNA but makes more errors. What might be defective?

Loss of proofreading is a candidate: a mismatch at the growing 3′ end would be less likely to be removed. But an error-prone polymerase could also be worse at selecting the correct incoming nucleotide. An error rate alone does not distinguish the two.

Follow-up: How would you separate those explanations in principle?

Listen for: Compare removal from a pre-existing mismatched end with discrimination between incoming nucleotides; these are different activities.

Why is one intermediate band after one replication round not decisive?

Both semiconservative and classic dispersive models can predict that band. The second round is more discriminating: distinct light and hybrid bands support semiconservative replication.

Follow-up: What would the conservative model predict at round one?

Listen for: Separate heavy and light bands, not a hybrid-only band.

Chapter 15

Transcription and cis/trans regulation

Separate the instruction from the molecule that reads it

The promoter is a DNA region that helps position transcription initiation; an operator is a regulatory DNA site at which a regulator can bind. An operator can overlap a promoter, but the terms describe different functions. A bacterial sigma factor contributes to promoter recognition by RNA polymerase. RNA polymerase reads the template 3′→5′ and synthesises RNA 5′→3′. For a specified gene the RNA matches the coding strand across the transcribed segment, with U in place of T, before processing changes the RNA. The choice of template depends on the direction of that gene, not which strand is drawn on top.

The lac operon: two inputs, two regulatory effects

In the standard E. coli model, LacI binds operator DNA and represses transcription when inducer is scarce. Allolactose, made from lactose, lowers LacI’s ability to occupy the operator. Under glucose limitation, cAMP–CAP can stimulate transcription, provided repression is relieved. Therefore the canonical strong-expression state has lactose available and glucose low. Think in terms of relative levels rather than a perfectly digital truth table: removing repression and supplying positive activation are two different events. A eukaryotic example with similar reasoning but different machinery is an enhancer bound by a transcription factor: the DNA element is cis-acting, while the mobile protein can act in trans. A cis element need not lie immediately next to the gene it regulates.

Worked case: two copies in one cell

Copy A drives a green reporter and has an operator change that prevents normal repressor binding. Copy B drives a red reporter and has an intact operator. A functional, diffusible repressor is present, and the simplified comparison keeps activation fixed so the reporters are otherwise detectable. In the absence of inducer, A escapes repression while B remains repressed. The change in A acts in cis: it changes the linked regulatory site. A loss of the diffusible repressor, by contrast, can affect both intact copies. Supplying functional repressor can restore repression of intact operators when the missing activity is a simple loss of function, but it cannot repair an operator that cannot bind it. A reporter difference still needs checks for copy number, transcript stability and reporter detection before it is assigned specifically to initiation.

Oral practice

Why can a repressor mutation act in trans, whereas an operator mutation usually acts in cis?

The repressor is a diffusible product that can reach multiple compatible DNA copies. The operator is part of a particular DNA molecule and directly controls linked transcription. A second intact operator does not replace the defective site on the first copy.

Follow-up: Would adding functional repressor rescue both defects?

Listen for: It can rescue a simple repressor loss at an intact site, but not restore binding to a site that cannot bind.

With lactose present, why might lac expression still be lower when glucose is abundant?

Lactose-derived inducer relieves LacI repression. Low glucose supports activation through cAMP–CAP. With abundant glucose, that positive input is weaker, so relief of repression is not the same as maximal transcription.

Follow-up: Is CAP an activator or an inducer in this explanation?

Listen for: CAP is the regulatory protein; cAMP is its ligand, and allolactose acts on LacI.

Two cells give different reporter fluorescence. Does that prove different transcription rates?

No. Fluorescence reflects reporter amount and state. Differences in transcript stability, translation, reporter maturation, degradation, cell number or construct copy number can alter the endpoint. I would measure evidence closer to initiation and check that the copies and readout were comparable.

Follow-up: Would measuring steady-state reporter RNA settle the initiation claim?

Listen for: No; RNA abundance depends on synthesis and loss. A measure of nascent transcription is closer to the claim.

Chapter 16

RNA processing, translation and RNA abundance

A gene, a transcript and a protein are different objects

Most nuclear RNA polymerase II protein-coding transcripts acquire a 5′ cap, are spliced and undergo 3′ cleavage followed by polyadenylation; there are exceptions. Splicing removes selected introns and joins exons. Exons can include untranslated regions, so exon does not mean protein-coding. Alternative splicing can change the coding sequence or only the untranslated parts. Cleavage and polyadenylation define a mature RNA end in the usual pathway; a stop codon terminates translation, not transcription. Bacteria generally lack this canonical processing pathway for their mRNAs, and transcription and translation can be coupled.

From codon to peptide

The ribosome moves along mRNA 5′→3′ and builds the peptide from its N terminus to its C terminus. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases attach amino acids to the corresponding tRNAs. In elongation, an incoming charged tRNA is selected at the A site, the growing peptide is transferred from the P-site tRNA onto the amino acid at the A site, and translocation moves the tRNAs through the ribosome. A release factor recognises a stop codon and promotes release of the peptide. In many bacteria, a Shine–Dalgarno interaction helps position the ribosome; many eukaryotic mRNAs instead use cap-dependent recruitment and scanning, with start-site context influencing recognition. Neither description covers every transcript.

Worked case: a measurement can merge isoforms

A gene has isoforms E1–E2–E3 and E1–E3. A qPCR assay entirely inside shared E3 can detect both isoforms, assuming both generate amplifiable cDNA and no other signals contribute. A properly validated exon-junction assay targeting E1–E3 can instead provide evidence for that skipped isoform. Imagine total gene RNA is unchanged, but the E1–E3 junction signal increases and the E1–E2 junction signal decreases. This supports a change in isoform composition under the stated assay assumptions, not an increase in total RNA. Compare changes within a validated junction assay rather than treating Cq differences between distinct assays as direct molecule counts. In sequencing data, junction reads can support connectivity, but sparse coverage or shared junctions can prevent unambiguous reconstruction of an entire isoform.

If protein drops while RNA stays constant: altered translation, faster protein degradation, or a change in the detected protein form can fit the observation. At steady state, equal RNA does not imply equal rates of RNA synthesis and decay. Start by naming the assay’s measured object before choosing a mechanistic explanation.

Oral practice

Can a single gene make two mature RNAs without changing its DNA?

Yes. The same primary transcript can be spliced in different ways, creating different exon junctions. These isoforms can differ in protein sequence or just in untranslated regions. The evidence should distinguish the relevant isoforms rather than simply measure a shared exon.

Follow-up: Would detecting one distinctive junction establish the entire isoform?

Listen for: Not necessarily; other distant junctions or transcript ends may differ.

An RT-qPCR signal is unchanged, but a protein band is half as strong. What is your first interpretation?

The measured RNA region appears unchanged and the measured protein signal is lower. Translation or protein turnover could explain it, as could a shift to a form the antibody detects less well. I would first check assay comparability and then ask whether protein production or loss changed.

Follow-up: What is one measurement that addresses protein loss rather than abundance alone?

Listen for: Follow target disappearance over time; distinguish a decay-rate measurement from another endpoint blot.

How do you tell a transcription start site from a translation start site?

The transcription start is the first nucleotide copied into RNA; the translation start is the codon at which the ribosome begins protein synthesis. An untranslated region can lie between them. The same distinction applies at the other end: a stop codon ends translation, not transcription.

Follow-up: With the same translation start, does skipping an internal segment of 90 coding nucleotides shift the downstream reading frame? What about 91?

Listen for: Assuming no other changes and that translation reaches the junction, removing 90 coding nucleotides preserves the downstream frame; removing 91 shifts it. A preserved frame does not imply preserved protein function.

Chapter 17

Protein targeting, trafficking and secretion

Trace the protein, not the compartment names

For a typical soluble mammalian secreted protein, translation starts on a cytosolic ribosome. As an N-terminal ER signal sequence emerges, signal recognition particle (SRP) recognises the emerging signal and directs the ribosome–nascent-chain complex to the ER. At the Sec61 translocon, synthesis continues with the growing chain entering the ER lumen. A cleavable signal peptide is removed in the common example shown. Protein folding and some modifications occur in the ER; many cargo proteins pass through the Golgi, where processing and sorting can continue, and then leave by vesicle fusion. A soluble protein does not have to cross the plasma membrane a second time: fusion opens the vesicular lumen to the extracellular space.

Read topology as a prediction

An internal signal anchor can remain in the membrane. A stop-transfer segment can also act as a membrane-spanning helix, but a cleaved signal peptide and a retained membrane anchor are not equivalent. For a model single-pass protein with a cleaved N-terminal signal peptide followed by a stop-transfer segment, the N-terminal domain enters the ER lumen and the downstream C-terminal tail stays cytosolic. Vesicular transport preserves this orientation: if the protein reaches the surface by the conventional route, the lumenal N-terminal domain becomes extracellular, while the C-terminal tail remains cytosolic. Do not generalise this orientation to every single-pass protein; different targeting signals can give different topology.

Worked case: less outside does not mean less made

A mammalian culture has the same steady-state RNA signal after a perturbation. A validated protein assay reports less target in the medium, while a cellular assay reports more cell-associated target after accounting for the number of viable cells. The pattern motivates a trafficking or release hypothesis, but a change in extracellular persistence or intracellular turnover could also contribute. Cell damage can release intracellular proteins and alter the medium independently of regulated secretion. A time-dependent comparison of intracellular and extracellular target, with a viability or leakage readout, addresses different alternatives. A single endpoint cannot isolate the secretion rate. This is a reasoning model for conventionally secreted proteins, not a claim that all extracellular proteins follow this route.

Oral practice

How does a soluble secreted protein get from a ribosome to the extracellular space?

Translation starts in the cytosol. An emerging ER signal recruits SRP; the ribosome docks and the chain enters the ER through a translocon. After folding and sorting in the secretory pathway, vesicle fusion releases the soluble cargo outside.

Follow-up: Does the protein cross the plasma membrane during release?

Listen for: No second crossing in this route; the vesicle lumen becomes continuous with the outside.

A single-pass reporter has its N terminus in the ER lumen and its C terminus in the cytosol. Which end will be exposed at the cell surface?

If the reporter follows the conventional vesicular route to the surface, the N terminus will face outside. The C terminus remains cytosolic because budding and fusion preserve membrane topology.

Follow-up: Why can an antibody against an extracellular tag bind intact cells, whereas a cytosolic tag is normally inaccessible?

Listen for: An intact plasma membrane separates the antibody from the cytosolic epitope; appropriate accessibility and expression controls matter.

Medium protein falls while cell-associated protein rises. Have you proved a secretion block?

No. Redistribution is compatible with a secretion defect, but extracellular loss and altered intracellular turnover can give similar endpoints. I would measure both compartments over time and account for viable cell number and leakage.

Follow-up: Is unchanged mRNA enough to rule out altered protein synthesis?

Listen for: No, translation can change without a change in RNA abundance.

Chapter 18

Enzyme kinetics and inhibition

The rate curve starts with a mechanism

For a simple single-substrate enzyme, E and S bind reversibly to form ES, and ES produces E and P. Measure the initial rate after the short transient, when the ES pool is approximately steady and product accumulation and substrate depletion can be neglected. The model gives v₀ = Vmax[S]/(Km + [S]). At [S] = Km, the rate is half of Vmax. At high substrate the enzyme approaches saturation. Vmax = kcat[E]total for this model when [E]total denotes active enzyme concentration, so enzyme amount affects Vmax, and kcat is turnover per active enzyme unit at saturation. Rate and substrate concentration have different units; Km has the units of concentration.

Why Km is not always affinity

For E + S ⇌ ES → E + P, the steady-state expression is Km = (koff + kcat)/kon; the binding dissociation constant is Kd = koff/kon. They are close in the rapid-equilibrium limit where turnover is slow compared with dissociation. Otherwise, a change in Km need not mean the same change in binding affinity. At low substrate, kcat/Km sets the slope of the rate curve once normalised to active enzyme concentration. Enzymes with cooperativity, substrate inhibition, or more complex reaction schemes need not follow the simple curve.

Recognise patterns, then test the model

In the ideal reversible competitive model, apparent Km increases and Vmax is unchanged. The ideal pure noncompetitive case lowers Vmax without changing Km; mixed inhibition need not leave Km unchanged. Ideal uncompetitive inhibition lowers apparent Km and Vmax by the same factor. In real data an incompletely sampled plateau, inactive enzyme, loss of linear initial conditions, or an interfering readout can make a different process look like inhibition. Measure initial rates across a useful substrate range, fit the untransformed rates to plausible models, and check residuals and uncertainty rather than choosing the mechanism from the apparent shape at one concentration. An endpoint absorbance is not an initial rate, and the absorbance of an ELISA calibrator is not itself an enzyme saturation experiment.

Worked calculation. If Vmax is 120 nM product per minute and Km is 20 µM, the simple model gives 60 nM/min at 20 µM and 90 nM/min at 60 µM. If an inhibitor doubles apparent Km to 40 µM without changing Vmax, the predicted rate at 20 µM falls to 40 nM/min. In the ideal competitive model the high-substrate curves approach the same Vmax.

Oral practice

What does Km mean, and when can you interpret it as a binding constant?

In the simple Michaelis–Menten model, Km is the substrate concentration at half Vmax. It includes both dissociation and catalysis. It approximates Kd when ES dissociates much faster than it turns over.

Follow-up: What units do Km and kcat have?

Listen for: Concentration and inverse time, respectively, not the same dimensions.

What pattern would support reversible competitive inhibition?

The initial-rate curve shifts right, so apparent Km rises, while the limiting rate stays the same at fixed active enzyme concentration. That supports competition under the model. I would compare fitted models over a substrate range rather than infer it from one point.

Follow-up: What if Vmax is lower but apparent Km is unchanged?

Listen for: The ideal pure noncompetitive pattern; loss of active enzyme can also lower Vmax, so the pattern is not proof of a binding mechanism.

Two preparations have the same Km but one has double the Vmax. Do the enzyme molecules work twice as fast?

Not necessarily. Vmax depends on both turnover and active enzyme amount. If active enzyme concentrations are equal and the model is appropriate, the preparation with double Vmax has double the turnover; otherwise enzyme amount remains an explanation.

Follow-up: Why use the early slope rather than product after a long incubation?

Listen for: Substrate depletion, product effects or enzyme instability can change the slope later.

Chapter 19

Plasmids: replication, maintenance and expression

A plasmid is a genetic replicon that can be maintained separately from the chromosome in a suitable host. Many bacterial plasmids are circular DNA molecules, but circularity is not the definition. Separate replication does not mean independence from the cell: plasmid replication draws on cellular machinery and resources. A vector is a tool for carrying genetic material; a plasmid can be used as a vector, but the terms are not interchangeable.

Read the map as a set of different jobs

An origin of replication is a region involved in initiating copying of the DNA molecule. A promoter helps position RNA polymerase to initiate transcription of a particular transcript. In our fictional reporter, a downstream coding region supplies the instructions for making the protein, while a transcription terminator is associated with ending transcription. A translation stop codon is distinct: it is read by the ribosome. A backbone is the vector portion distinguished from the inserted DNA; those labels are relative to how the construct was assembled or described. The map summarises annotation and orientation, not proof that any element is intact or active in the sample.

Why copying a plasmid is not enough to keep it

DNA replication increases the number of copies. Inheritance asks a different question: after division, did each daughter receive a copy? Some plasmids have partition systems that help distribute copies to daughter cells. That is inheritance within a lineage, not evidence of transfer between cells. Even if the population-average copy number is stable, the number per cell can vary and some cells can lack the plasmid. Copy number, usually discussed per cell or per chromosome equivalent, is not the fraction of cells carrying it. State the denominator; a chromosome reference can itself vary per cell with growth state.

Worked case: the same mean can hide loss

In toy population A, all ten cells carry four plasmid copies each. In population B, five cells carry eight copies each and the other five carry none. Both populations average four copies per cell. But all cells carry the plasmid in A, compared with half in B. A bulk measurement of total plasmid molecules divided by cell number cannot distinguish these two cases. A measurement that resolves individual cells can address the distribution, provided its ability to detect low copy counts is understood.

Why more reporter signal does not necessarily mean a stronger promoter

A cell can contain more reporter RNA simply because it has more DNA templates. At steady state, RNA abundance also depends on transcript loss. A protein signal additionally reflects translation, protein maturation or degradation, and the measurement itself. Do not use fluorescence alone to identify which step changed. Shao and colleagues used separate single-cell readouts for plasmid DNA, RNA and protein, illustrating why one signal can be insufficient to assign a cause.

Keep three questions separate at the panel

Is the DNA present and what sequence does it carry? Is the reporter expressed? Does that expression account for the phenotype? A read spanning a region supports sequence identity there; it does not measure expression. RNA, protein and a functional readout answer different questions. A correct plasmid map can help you formulate a prediction, but an actual observation and its controls are needed to evaluate the prediction.

Oral practice

If you can detect plasmid DNA but no reporter protein, has the origin failed?

Not necessarily. The origin concerns replication, whereas reporter production depends on transcription, translation and protein detection. Detectable DNA does not tell me whether the reporter RNA or protein was produced. I would first separate the DNA, RNA and protein measurements.

Follow-up: Could a single snapshot of plasmid DNA prove that it is being replicated?

Listen for: No. Presence at one time point is not a measurement of new synthesis or inheritance.

Can two bacterial populations with the same mean plasmid copy number differ in plasmid retention?

Yes. A population in which each cell carries four copies and one in which half carry eight and half carry none both average four copies. The mean loses information about the fraction of carriers and the single-cell distribution.

Follow-up: What additional kind of information would distinguish your two examples?

Listen for: A carrier fraction or a reliable single-cell distribution, not merely another bulk average.

Two reporters differ in fluorescence. Have you measured promoter strength?

Fluorescence is a downstream output. Higher signal could reflect more DNA copies, changes in RNA loss, translation or protein state. I would compare evidence for DNA amount, reporter RNA and protein before assigning a change to transcription per template.

Follow-up: If steady-state RNA per DNA copy is higher, does that by itself prove faster transcription?

Listen for: No. Slower RNA loss could also increase steady-state abundance per template.

Chapter 20

From an observed signal to a biological explanation

An assay usually observes one layer of a system. A sequence suggests what might fold or bind; an image records a cell state; a concentration measures the size of a pool. These signals become useful evidence when you say which claim they support and which alternative they cannot distinguish. This chapter uses three unrelated teaching examples to practise that step.

DNA sequence, folded structure and regulatory consequence

A putative structural motif is a sequence pattern, not a direct observation of folding. A separate readout can support folding in a particular context; a functional comparison is needed to connect the structure to expression. Bulk averages also discard which states occurred together. Imagine 100 DNA molecules, and sites A and B each occupied on 50. In one possible population A and B are occupied on the same 50 molecules; in another they are occupied on different sets of 50. Both have 50% occupancy at each site, but co-occupancy is 50% in one population and 0% in the other. Co-occurrence on DNA is informative but does not on its own identify a direct physical interaction between the bound factors.

Image features predict an endpoint, not necessarily a mechanism

An image profile summarises features such as shape, texture or the spatial distribution of fluorescence. Similar profiles can come from different underlying processes. In a fictional experiment, all control wells are imaged on Monday and all treated wells on Tuesday. A classifier can learn the imaging day or other shared run effects rather than biology. Splitting cells randomly from the same wells across training and test sets does not create an independent test. If all treated samples and controls were collected on separate days with no overlap, modelling alone cannot separate those effects from those data. A better study has both conditions represented across independent batches; validate on whole held-out units relevant to the intended use, such as an independent run. Cells measured in one well are observations, not individually assigned treatment replicates.

A metabolite pool is not a flux

Concentration is amount per volume. Flux is a rate, for example an amount per unit time normalised to a stated quantity. In an idealised fixed-volume system, production and consumption at 2 units per minute leave the pool unchanged. Production and consumption at 10 units per minute also leave it unchanged. The pool can therefore have the same size with different turnover. Even a time series of the pool describes net accumulation or loss, not both underlying rates separately. Data that track how a marker moves through the system can help distinguish otherwise compatible rates, subject to the model. This is the same reasoning as distinguishing how much protein is present from its rate of production.

Choose the reference before explaining a stress response

In a fictional yeast dataset, culture A ends at 100 units without mild salt stress and 80 with it; B ends at 60 without stress and 55 with it. A is denser under stress, but on the absolute scale A drops by 20 and B by 5 relative to their own baselines. On that scale the difference in changes, A minus B, is (80 − 100) − (55 − 60) = −15 units. These endpoints do not show the growth rate, viability, or a specific protective mechanism. If you care instead about the proportion retained, state that estimand and justify it. Define the effect scale and match the measure to the question before declaring that one culture is protected.

Oral practice

Two DNA sites are each occupied on half the molecules. Must the sites be co-occupied?

No. The same marginal occupancies can come from the same molecules or disjoint sets. A joint measurement per molecule can tell those cases apart.

Follow-up: Would observing co-occupancy prove direct contact between the proteins?

Listen for: No. Shared context or another factor could explain it.

A predictive model separates treated and control cells, but treatment is confounded with imaging day. Is the test convincing?

Not for a treatment effect. Day and treatment cannot be distinguished, and random cell splits can share the same wells and run effects. I would want both groups on multiple days and a test holding out the relevant independent units.

Follow-up: If the treatment was assigned to wells, are hundreds of cells hundreds of independent treatments?

Listen for: No. Identify the unit of assignment and any dependence between wells, for example common donors.

An unchanged metabolite concentration is observed over time. Must both production and consumption be unchanged?

No. An unchanged pool means inputs and outputs balance under the model, not that their individual rates are unchanged. Both can increase by the same amount.

Follow-up: Does a higher final cell density establish better protection under stress?

Listen for: Not without baselines and a stated comparison. Density, viability and a mechanistic claim are different outcomes.
